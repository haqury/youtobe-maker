## The Cabin Door

You step through the narrow door of your cabin, and it closes behind you with a soft, padded click. The sound is small and complete. Behind you is the corridor, with its long quiet floor and its low strips of light. In front of you is the room that belongs to you, and only to you, on this ship.

The cabin is not large. It does not need to be. The ceiling is close enough that you could reach it, and the walls curve in a little at the corners, the way the inside of a shell curves. Everything has been fitted to a place. A narrow shelf runs along one wall with a small rail to keep your things where you leave them. A folding desk is tucked flat against the panel, and a single stool sits beneath it. A hook by the door holds your jacket, and you hang it there now, and smooth the sleeve once with your hand.

You are on a research ship, and the ship has been at work for a long time. The work is done for today. The instruments have been set, the logs have been written, and the crew has said its quiet good nights in the corridors, one by one, until the voices thinned and stopped. Tonight the ship is flying itself. The autopilot holds the course, steady and patient, the way it has held it through many nights before this one. The ship is fine. Everything aboard is exactly where it should be. Nothing needs you, and nothing is waiting for you to notice it.

You have no duty tonight. You say this to yourself slowly, and you feel how true it is. No watch to stand. No panel to check. No one to meet at the start of the next shift until the ship's morning, which is still far away. The hours ahead are yours, and they are soft and wide and empty, and you do not have to fill them with anything.

You sit down on the edge of the stool and take off your boots. They are heavy, work boots with thick soles, and they have walked a great many corridors. You loosen the laces one at a time. You ease the first boot from your foot and set it beside the desk, and then the second. Your feet rest on the floor in your socks. The floor is smooth and has a faint give to it, like the cover of a well worn book, and it is pleasantly cool for a moment and then no longer cool at all.

You look around the cabin once more, the way you might look around a room in a house you love, just to take it in. The bunk waits against the far wall, narrow and built into the frame, with a rail along its edge and a thick fitted mattress. The linen on it is pale and crisp and turned down at one corner, as though someone expected you. Beside the bunk is a small ledge, and on the ledge sits the cabin lamp, not yet lit. It is a little brass thing with a round shade of frosted glass. Above the bunk, set deep into the wall, is a wide round porthole, and beyond it there is only the dark, and a faint movement of colors that you will come to in a moment.

You stand, and the stool slides back under the desk with a small, soft sound. You cross the cabin in your socks. It takes only three steps, and each step is quiet. You stop by the bunk and rest your hand on the cool top of the rail, and you feel the metal warm slowly under your palm. Then you reach for the little lamp on the ledge, and you turn its small brass key.

The lamp wakes without a sound. It does not flare. It brightens the way an ember brightens when you breathe on it, from nothing to a low honey glow. The warm light spreads across the ledge and the edge of the linen and the curve of the wall, and the cabin becomes a smaller, closer, gentler place. The corners fall into soft shadow. The shelf, the desk, the hook with your jacket, all of them take on a golden edge, and none of them is sharp.

You stand there a moment in that warmth, and you listen.

## The Hum

There is a sound in the cabin, and it has been there all along. You have simply not listened to it until now. It is the quiet hum of the engines, far off in the body of the ship, carried to you through the walls and the floor and the frame of the bunk.

It is a low sound, and it is very even. It does not rise. It does not fall. It is like the purr of a large, contented animal sleeping somewhere in the next room, one that has been breathing in the same slow rhythm since before you were born. You can feel it more than you hear it. It sits in the soles of your feet when you stand on the floor. It rests in your hand when you lay your palm against the wall.

You lay your palm against the wall, and there it is, a very faint trembling, so fine that it feels like warmth. The panel is smooth and slightly cool, and under the smoothness there is that steady, patient purr. You hold your hand there and let the hum travel up your wrist and into your arm. It is a comfortable feeling. It is the feeling of being carried by something strong that has no wish to let you fall.

The hum does not change. This is the best thing about it. Whatever you do in the cabin, whether you move or sit still, whether you speak or stay silent, the sound goes on exactly as before. It never spikes. It never stutters. It has no sharp edges at all. It is a long, round, unbroken tone, and it fills the cabin the way water fills a bowl, quietly, to the very rim, and then simply stays.

You take your hand from the wall and stand listening to the cabin. Beneath the hum there are smaller sounds, and they are all kind. The soft hush of air through the grille. The faint tick of the lamp as its brass settles into the warmth. The whisper of your own socks on the floor as you shift your weight. Each of these sounds lies on top of the hum like a thread laid on a broad, dark cloth, and none of them disturbs it.

You walk to the shelf and run your fingers along the rail. The metal hums under your touch, very faintly, like a sleeping cat under a hand. You find your small folded sleep shirt tucked beside a mug and a book you have not opened in days. You take the shirt down and hold it against your chest. It is soft and has been washed many times. You change slowly, with no hurry in any movement, and you fold the day's clothes and lay them on the stool.

The shirt settles around you like a second, kinder skin. You stand a moment in the warm lamplight, and the hum carries on through the floor, and you feel the tiredness in your body turn from something heavy into something soft. It is not a tiredness that has to be fought. It is a tiredness that has been waiting patiently for you to stop and let it in, and now you let it in.

You rest your forehead for a moment against the cool panel of the wall, and you let the hum run through your skull, a long, low, patient tone. It reaches somewhere behind your eyes and smooths something there. It reaches the back of your neck and the top of your spine. It reaches all the small knots you carried in from the day, and one by one they ease. You stand with your eyes closed and your forehead against the wall, and the whole ship purrs around you.

When you open your eyes again, the cabin is a little warmer, or perhaps you are. The lamp glows on the ledge. The linen waits on the bunk. And in the wall above the bed the round window is faintly alive with color.

## The Porthole

You turn toward the porthole. It is wide and round and set deep in the wall, and its glass is thick and clear, with a soft, polished rim of metal all around it. Beyond the glass is the open dark, a deep and velvety dark, and you look into it with no fear at all, as you might look into a quiet garden at night from a warm kitchen window.

And in the dark, there is color.

It begins at one side of the window, a faint stain of rose and violet, as soft as breath on glass. It is not a shape you can name. It has no edges. It is a slow, wide mist, lit from somewhere far behind itself, and it spreads across the dark like ink spreading in a bowl of water. At its heart the color deepens to a warm plum, and at its edges it thins to a pale and silvery pink, and then to nothing.

You step closer and sit on the edge of the bunk so that the porthole is just above and in front of you. The lamp is small and warm at your elbow, and the glass of the window gives back a faint reflection of it, a tiny golden point floating in the mist like a lantern held by someone far away. You do not look at the reflection. You look through it, into the slow bright cloud beyond.

The mist drifts. It does not hurry. It moves across the window the way a cloud moves across a summer sky on a day with no wind, so slowly that you cannot see it move, and yet each time you look again it has gone a little further. The rose slides toward the edge of the glass. Behind it comes a pool of soft teal, the color of deep water in the shade, and then a thread of amber, thin as a hair, and then a wide quiet sweep of blue.

You do not know the names of these clouds, and you do not need to. They are only colors, only soft light, passing by on a long and unhurried journey, just as you are. Some are pale and airy, like silk hung out to dry in a slow wind. Some are dense and deep, with a glow at the center like a coal beneath ash. They do not ask anything of you. They do not need to be understood. They are simply there to be seen, and to be forgotten, and to be replaced by the next.

A fine dust of tiny stars glitters through the mist, so faint and so many that they are less like points and more like a shimmer, the way sunlight sparkles on the surface of a pond. They do not blink. They do not hurry. They hang behind the colors like grains of sugar scattered on dark velvet, and the colors drift in front of them in slow, silent veils.

The glow from the window spills into the cabin. It lays a faint wash of color across the wall beside the bunk, a pale violet that slowly turns to blue and then to a soft green. It touches the edge of the linen and the rim of the mug and the brass of the lamp, and each of them takes on a trace of the hue for a moment before the next hue arrives. The cabin breathes in color. The warm gold of the lamp stays steady in the middle of it all, small and calm and kind, like a hearth in a house with colored windows.

The hum goes on beneath the window, even and low. The colors move above it. Together they make a single quiet music of the body and the eyes, one that has no tune and no end. The hum is the floor of it, and the colors are the sky, and you rest in the space between them with nothing at all to do.

## The Blanket

When you are ready, you reach for the blanket.

It lies folded at the foot of the bunk, a heavy wool blanket the color of oatmeal, with a broad stripe of soft gray along each edge. It has been washed so many times that the wool has become soft as a cloud. You lift it in both hands and feel its weight, a good, comforting weight, the weight of something made to keep you warm. You hold it for a moment against your chest and breathe in its quiet scent of wool and clean air.

You shake it out once, gently, and it opens in the lamplight like a slow, wide wing. The stripes catch the glow. The colors from the window slide over its folds, a pale blue, then a trace of rose. You lay the blanket across the linen, and you smooth it with the flat of your hand, from one end to the other, until there are no wrinkles left to trouble you. The bunk is made. It is ready, and it is yours.

You slide your legs beneath the blanket and settle your feet against the soft end of the mattress. The linen is cool for a moment, as fresh linen always is, and then the warmth of your body slowly fills it, and it becomes the exact warmth of rest. You pull the blanket up over your knees, and then your hips, and then your chest, and you tuck its edge beneath your chin, and you feel the weight of it settle over you like a long, kind hand.

You lie back against the pillow. It is flat and soft and shaped by many nights, and it takes the weight of your head with no effort at all. The linen under your cheek is smooth. The wool at your chin is warm. Your arms lie outside the blanket for a moment, heavy and loose, and then you draw them under, and the warmth closes around you, and you feel the day finally let go.

You are lying on your back now, and the porthole is above you and a little to one side. You can see the whole of it from the pillow, a round window full of slowly moving color. The glass is a quiet frame. Inside it, a long soft veil of green is sliding into a deep violet, and the violet is learning to be blue, and the blue is thinning into a silver haze with tiny stars scattered through it like salt.

The lamp is small and warm beside your head. It sits on the ledge at the edge of your sight, a low golden glow. You can feel its gentle heat on the side of your face, as faint as the sun on a winter morning. You do not need to turn it down. It is already small enough, already soft enough, a little round of gold in a cabin full of drifting colors.

The hum is in the bunk. It comes up through the frame and into the mattress, and you feel it in your back and your legs, a deep, low, steady purr, the sound of a ship that knows its way. It is the same hum as before. It has not changed at all. It has not even slowed. The ship carries you and your blanket and your pillow through the dark, and the sound it makes while doing so is the sound of a long, peaceful breath.

You lie there. The colors drift. The hum stays.

You feel your feet grow heavy and warm. You feel the weight of the wool settle on your shins and your thighs. You feel your belly rise and fall, slow and soft, beneath the blanket. You feel your hands, each of them lying open on the linen, and the small, quiet weight of each finger. You feel your shoulders sink into the mattress, and your neck sink into the pillow, and the back of your head sink into the dark of the cloth.

You feel your face soften. The space between your eyebrows smooths itself. Your eyelids grow heavy, and then heavier, and you let them close for a little while, and then you open them again and see the colors still moving, and the lamp still glowing, and the hum still running through the frame of the bunk. You close them again. They stay closed a little longer this time.

When you open them once more, the window has turned a soft and glowing amber, with a long pale ribbon of blue across its middle. It is the color of honey held up to a candle. It spills across the ceiling above you and runs slowly down the wall, and it sits across the blanket in a long warm band, and the whole cabin is gold and quiet and still.

You watch the amber fade into rose, and the rose into violet, and the violet into a deep, rich, velvety blue. You watch the little stars shimmer and shift behind it. You watch the slow edge of the cloud pass by the glass and go on into the dark beyond. And you do not think about it. You only let it be there, and you let it go, and you let the next one come.

## Drifting

The colors are slower now. Or you are. It is hard to tell the difference. Everything in the cabin has become one long, easy drift, the blanket and the hum and the lamp and the slow, soft glow in the glass.

Your breathing is long. It rises and falls. The blanket rises and falls with it.

The lamp is warm. The linen is soft. The hum is low.

You watch a pale green mist come into the window. It is thin and quiet. It lies across the stars like a veil. It passes slowly, and it goes.

You close your eyes. You open them. The window is rose.

The blanket is heavy and warm. Your hands are loose. Your feet are warm.

The hum goes on. It does not change. It does not need to.

A long slow wave of blue comes into the glass. It fills the porthole softly, edge to edge. It lights the ceiling. It lights the wall. It lights the folds of the wool, and then it fades.

You are not thinking now. You are only here. Here in the bunk, here in the warmth, here in the soft blue and gold.

Your eyes close. They stay closed.

You hear the hum. It is a long low tone. It is the sound of the ship. It is the sound of the night.

You feel the blanket. You feel the linen. You feel the soft weight of your own breath.

The color on your eyelids is violet. Then it is rose. Then it is dim gold.

The lamp ticks once, softly, and is still.

The ship flies on. It does not need you. It knows the way. It carries the colors past the glass, and it carries the hum through the walls, and it carries you, gently, through the long dark.

Your breath is slow. Your body is warm. Your mind is quiet.

The window glows. The window dims. The window glows again, a faint, far, silver blue.

You do not see it. You are almost gone.

The hum, and the warmth, and the wool.

The hum, and the warmth.

The hum.

The ship keeps flying. The colors keep drifting. The lamp keeps its small gold light on the ledge, and the blanket keeps its weight, and the linen keeps its cool soft quiet.

You sleep.

Nothing is asked of you. Nothing is needed.

You sleep, and the ship goes on.
