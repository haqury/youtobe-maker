## Chapter 1: The Hammock Under the Glass

You are already lying in a hammock, and the hammock is already holding you. There is nothing to arrange and nothing to decide. The long walk through the garden is finished, and the heavy gate is far behind you now. You are inside a glass greenhouse, deep in a warm green room that has been waiting for you, and you are exactly where you are meant to be.

Above you, the roof is made of glass. Hundreds of narrow panes are set into a pale iron frame, each one slightly different from the next, each one holding a little of the grey evening light. Tropical rain is falling on that glass. You can hear it clearly, a steady drum, soft and wide and even, like a thousand fingertips resting and lifting, resting and lifting. The rain does not hurry. It has been falling for a long time, and it will go on falling long after you have stopped listening.

You are dry. Your clothes are dry, your hair is dry, and the cloth beneath you is dry and a little warm. The rain stays on the other side of the glass, where it belongs, and you stay here on this side, where the air is gentle. You can feel how good it is to be dry while the rain is so near. The sound is close enough to fill the room, and the glass is strong enough to keep the room quiet inside the sound.

Let your weight settle into the hammock. It is woven from soft, thick cord, wide enough to cradle your shoulders and your hips at once. The cord curves up on both sides of you, so you rest in a long shallow cup. The hammock hangs from two iron hooks set into old beams, and the beams are dark and smooth from many years of warm air. The hooks do not creak. The cord does not slip. The hammock simply holds, the way it has always held, and you let it.

The greenhouse is large, but it does not feel empty. Broad leaves rise on every side of you, green and deep and glossy, some as wide as doors. They lean over the hammock in a loose, friendly ring, so that you lie in a small sheltered clearing inside the larger room. Between the leaves you can see stone paths, curved and narrow, and low beds of dark earth, and clay pots stacked beside a wooden bench. A watering can rests on its side near the bench, its brass spout dull and warm. Everything is quiet and tidy and still, apart from the rain.

Breathe in slowly. The air tastes soft. It is warm, and it carries a little moisture, the way air does after a long summer storm. Breathe out, and let the breath leave you unhurried. The greenhouse seems to breathe along with you. Somewhere in the warm air, a leaf shifts its weight and settles again.

The cloth of the hammock smells faintly of clean cotton and warm earth. There is a folded blanket across your legs, thick and soft, the colour of moss. You did not notice it arrive, but it is here, and it is just heavy enough to feel like a hand resting on you. Your toes are warm beneath it. Your knees are warm. The warmth moves slowly upward through the blanket and settles into your hips and your belly, and your belly rises and falls with the easy rhythm of the rain.

Nothing needs you tonight. The garden outside is watered by the sky. The plants in here are watered, too, and fed, and content. The greenhouse keeps its own time, and tonight its time is slow. There is only the hammock, and the glass, and the long, steady sound of rain, and the warm green room folded around you like a quiet coat.

## Chapter 2: The Plants That Hold the Warmth

Turn your attention gently toward the plants, because the plants in this greenhouse are not ordinary ones. They are warmth-loving plants, grown from old seed, and they have gathered here for the same reason you have. They love the warm air, and they love the sound of rain on glass, and they love to be left alone in the soft evening to do their slow, patient growing.

Near your left shoulder, a tall plant rises from a wide clay pot. Its stem is thick and pale green, and its leaves open outward like hands held open to catch the damp air. Each leaf is broad and ribbed, and along the edge of each leaf a faint glow has begun to gather. It is a gentle glow, the colour of a coal that has been resting in the ashes for an hour. It is not bright. It does not flash or pulse or call out. It is only a soft amber warmth inside the green, rising and falling very slowly, as if the leaf were breathing light the way you are breathing air.

This is gentle magic, the kind that asks nothing of you. The plant glows because it is warm and content, and because the rain on the glass pleases it. That is all. There is nothing in the glow that wants you to look closer, and nothing in it that wants you to look away. You can rest your eyes on it, or let your eyes drift past it, and the plant is equally at peace.

A little farther along the path, a cluster of smaller plants sits in a long wooden trough. Their leaves are round and thick, shaped like soft green coins, and a few of them are glowing, too. They glow in no particular order. One leaf here, one leaf there, a faint honey colour that rises and fades. They look like the last few embers in a banked hearth, scattered across a bed of dark green ash. When one of them dims, another brightens nearby, so that the trough always holds a little warm light, though no single leaf keeps it for long.

Above the trough, a vine hangs from the iron frame in long loops. Its leaves are heart shaped and silky, and the undersides of the leaves carry a pale, silvery shimmer, like the inside of a shell. When the vine sways in the warm air, the shimmer drifts across the leaves in slow ripples. The vine moves only a little. Nothing disturbs it. It simply responds to the air that rises from the warm earth below, and the air rises gently, and the vine sways gently in return.

Everywhere in the greenhouse, the plants are at rest. Some of them fold their leaves inward for the evening, the way hands fold in a lap. Some of them lean toward the warm pipes that run beneath the benches. A fern near the path unrolls one tight green curl, and then stops, and holds the shape, and waits. The pace of this room is the pace of growing things, which is the slowest and kindest pace there is.

You are among friends here, though the friends do not speak. They do not need to. The plants hold the warmth of the room the way a pile of stones holds the heat of the sun long after the sun is gone. Each leaf stores a little of the day, and gives it back slowly through the night. That is why the air feels so soft. It is the breath of many leaves, warm and green and patient.

The plants do not need you to name them or to understand them. They have been growing in this room for longer than you can know, and they will go on growing when you are asleep. They are a little like old neighbours, who are glad when you are near and also glad when you are quiet. Whatever you feel toward them, they feel something warm in return, the slow, green kind of warmth that does not ask for anything.

Let the soft amber glow stay in the corner of your sight. Let it rise and fade, rise and fade, in the rhythm of the rain. Your eyes do not need to follow it. It will be there, gently, whether you watch or not. And with every slow breath, your body grows a little heavier in the hammock, and a little warmer, and a little more at home.

## Chapter 3: Damp Sweet Air and Warm Earth

The air in this greenhouse has a smell, and the smell is one of the kindest things about it. Breathe in through your nose, slowly, and notice what the air carries. It is damp and sweet, and underneath the sweetness lies the deep, dark smell of earth. It is the smell of soil after rain, rich and cool and full of quiet life. It is the smell of a garden at the end of a long day, when everything that grows has been given water and has taken its fill.

There is a green smell, too. It rises from the leaves, fresh and clean and a little bright, like a stem that has just been snapped between two fingers. It is not sharp. It is a soft green, the kind you might remember from a summer afternoon beside a stream, when the grass was cool and the shade was deep. The green smell and the earth smell mix together in the warm air, and the mixture is calm and full and slow.

Somewhere nearby, a flower has opened for the evening. You cannot see it from the hammock, but you can smell it, a faint, sweet thread in the air, like honey diluted in warm water. It comes and goes with the movement of the air. When it comes, it is soft and round. When it goes, the earth and the green fill the space where it was. You do not have to find the flower. You can simply let the sweetness pass through, and it will pass, and it will come again.

Breathe in, and feel the damp air fill your chest. It is not heavy. It is the kind of air that feels like a warm cloth laid across your face, gentle and soft. It moistens your lips and your throat. It makes every breath easy and wide, as if the air itself had been made to be breathed slowly. Breathe out, and let your shoulders fall. Breathe in again, and let the air carry you a little deeper into rest.

The hammock holds your weight, and it holds it well. Notice how little effort the holding takes. The cord is thick and strong, woven by patient hands from fibre that has been soaked and dried and woven again, and it has no thought of letting you go. The iron hooks are sunk deep into the beams. The beams are anchored to the great stone walls of the greenhouse. The walls rest on the ground, and the ground goes down and down into the quiet dark. You are held by all of it, one strong thing leaning on the next, all the way to the earth itself.

Because the hammock holds you, your body can do something it rarely does. It can stop holding itself. Let your jaw loosen, and let your tongue rest in your mouth. Let your forehead smooth out, as if a warm hand had passed over it. Let the muscles around your eyes grow slack. Let your neck sink into the cloth. You do not have to keep your head up or your shoulders squared or your belly drawn in. The hammock takes all of it, and gives you back only comfort.

Listen once more to the rain. It is a steady drum on the glass, never louder, never softer than it should be. It is the kind of rain that falls for hours over warm country, soaking the soil, filling the streams, feeding every root. It is not hail, and it will not be hail. The glass is quiet beneath it. The panes do not rattle. The iron frame does not shiver. The rain simply falls, and the glass simply receives it, and the room beneath stays warm and calm and dry.

Let your hands grow heavy at your sides. Let your fingers uncurl, one at a time. Let your breath lengthen until each breath is a small slow wave, rising and falling, rising and falling, with the same unhurried rhythm as the rain. The sweet air moves in and out of you. The earth smell settles into your chest. The green smell softens your mind. You are breathing the breath of the greenhouse, and the greenhouse is breathing yours.

There is no season in here. It is neither winter nor summer, only the warm middle of a long, green evening. The time that passes is measured in raindrops and leaf shadows and slow, even breaths. You have all the time you need, and you do not have to spend it on anything. You can let it pass through you, the way the damp air passes through the open vents, and be quietly glad that it is here.

## Chapter 4: Water Running Down the Panes

Let your hands rest on the edge of the hammock now. Find the place where the cord curves up beside your ribs, and lay your palms along it, or let your fingers curl over it loosely. The cord is thick and a little rough, and it is warm from your own body. It feels like the rim of a small boat floating on a very calm lake. Your hands do not hold it. They simply rest there, and the cord rests under them, and neither of you needs to move.

Turn your eyes up toward the glass. The rain has settled into a long, slow rhythm, and the water on the panes has begun to run in paths. Each pane has its own small stream. A bead of water gathers near the top, swells until it is too heavy to stay, and then slides downward, joining other beads on the way. It leaves a thin bright trail behind it, which fades as the next bead follows. The paths are never quite straight. They bend and wander and meet, and then they part again, like tiny rivers on a map of a very small country.

The water makes a very soft sound as it moves. It is not quite a sound at all. It is a faint, slippery whisper beneath the drumming of the rain, a hush within a hush. You can hear it if you listen very closely, and you can let it go if you do not. Either way, the whisper is there, a gentle companion to the sound of the storm.

Now let your eyes drift down from the roof to the room, and find the lantern. It hangs from a hook on the beam above the path, a short way from your hammock. It is an old brass lantern with a rounded glass chimney, and inside it burns one small, steady flame. The flame is the colour of honey and warm bread. It does not flicker much. It leans a little, and straightens, and leans again, in time with the slow breathing of the greenhouse.

The lantern is the one warm light in the room. Its glow falls on the stone path in a soft round pool, and on the edges of the nearest leaves, and on the clay pots beside the bench. It touches the wet panes above it, too, and each bead of water on the glass catches a tiny gold point, so that the roof seems scattered with sleepy stars. The light is quiet and low. It is the kind of light that makes everything it touches look a little softer than it is.

You can look at the lantern for as long as you like. Its flame is small and kind. It has no wish to be brighter, and no wish to be dimmer. It burns the way a quiet fire burns on a winter evening, giving what it has and asking nothing back. When your eyes grow tired, you can let them close, and the lantern will go on glowing behind your eyelids, a soft warm spot in the dark.

Listen again to the water on the glass, and the drum of the rain, and the faint breathing of the leaves. These sounds are all one sound now, a wide, slow, soothing sound that fills the greenhouse from floor to roof. It has no beginning that you can find, and no end that you need to wait for. It simply is, the way the sea is, or the night. You are resting in the middle of it, with your hands on the edge of the hammock and your breath rising and falling like a tide.

Notice how your hands feel now. They are heavier than before, and warmer, and the cord beneath them feels almost like part of you. Notice how your arms have loosened to the elbow, and then to the shoulder, and then to the long muscles along your spine. Notice that nothing in you is holding on. The hammock holds, and the glass holds, and the lantern holds its small gold light. You are free to let go of everything else.

## Chapter 5: Sleep Among the Leaves

Now the greenhouse grows quieter. The rain is still falling. It will go on falling. But your attention is soft now, and the sound has become the background of all things, the way the sea is the background of a house beside it.

The hammock rocks a little. It is hardly a movement at all. It is the smallest sway, the kind a breath makes, and then it is still again. The cord is warm. The blanket is warm. Your body is warm and heavy and wide.

The lantern burns. The leaves glow, soft and amber. The glass runs with water. The earth smells sweet.

You are dry. You are held. You are staying here.

Let your eyelids grow heavy. They are very heavy now. There is nothing to look at, and nothing to find. The leaves are green in the dark. The rain is soft on the roof. The room is quiet, and kind, and yours.

Breathe in. Breathe out. The air is warm. The air is damp and sweet. It smells of earth and green leaves.

Your hands rest on the cord. Your fingers are loose. Your arms are heavy. Your shoulders have sunk into the cloth, and they do not rise again.

The rain drums on the glass. It is steady. It is slow. It falls the way it has always fallen, over the leaves and the stones and the dark soil.

A leaf glows near the roof. It brightens, and fades. It brightens, and fades. It is a small ember in the green.

Your jaw is soft. Your brow is smooth. Your thoughts are slow and few, like raindrops sliding down one pane. One thought runs down the glass. Another follows. Then the glass is clear.

The lantern holds its light. The flame leans, and rests. The path below it glows, gold and still.

Your breath is long. Your breath is easy. Each one comes and goes like a wave on a quiet shore.

You are in the hammock. You are under the glass. You are inside, and you stay inside.

The rain falls on. The leaves breathe. The earth is warm.

Your feet are warm. Your legs are heavy. Your back is held. Your belly rises, and falls.

The sound of the rain is wide and soft. It fills the room. It fills your sleep.

Somewhere a drop slides down a pane. It is slow. It is bright. It reaches the iron, and rests.

The glow in the leaf is faint now. The lantern is low and gold. The dark between them is warm.

You are sinking. You are soft. You are carried.

The rain keeps on. The glass keeps still. The hammock keeps you.

Warm air. Green leaves. Gentle light.

Your breath is slow. Your body is heavy. The night is deep.

The rain is falling. You are sleeping. You are safe.

The glass is quiet. The leaves are still. The lantern burns.

Rest now. Rest.

Sleep.
