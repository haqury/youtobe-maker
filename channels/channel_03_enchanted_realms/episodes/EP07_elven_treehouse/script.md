## Chapter 1: High in the Old Crowns

It is night, and you are high in the crowns of an old forest. You are not climbing and you are not looking for anything. You have already arrived. The hidden treehouse is all around you, and it has been waiting for you the way a good chair waits by a fire, patient and quietly glad.

Look at where you are. You are standing on a wide floor of pale planks, and the planks were laid by careful hands a very long time ago. They fit together so closely that the seams are only thin lines, soft and silvery, like the lines on the back of an old hand. Under your feet the wood is steady. It does not creak in a way that asks for attention. It only gives a small, friendly sound now and then, the sound of a house settling in around you.

The house grows out of the great tree itself. A thick trunk rises through the middle of the room, wide as a round table, its bark deep and ridged and gentle to the touch. Around the trunk the walls curve outward, made of honey-colored boards that follow the shape of the branches instead of fighting it. Nothing here is straight for the sake of being straight. Every line bends a little, the way a river bends, because the elves who built this place listened to the tree and let the tree decide.

You rest one palm against the trunk. The bark is cool at the surface and warm underneath, and you can feel a very slow life moving inside it. It is not a beat like a heart. It is wider than that, and slower, a long patient hum that has been going since before anyone you know was born. The tree is old, and it is calm, and it does not mind that you are here. You are a small guest in a very large and very kind home.

Now notice the sway. The whole treehouse moves a little, so little that at first you might think it is you who is moving. The branches that hold the house are wide and strong, and the night wind touches the crown from far above, and the crown answers with a slow lean to one side, and then a slow lean back. The floor goes with it, gently, the way a cradle goes. You feel it in your knees and in your ankles and in the middle of your back, a soft rocking that asks nothing of you.

You are held. The great limbs under the floor are thicker than a dozen of you lying side by side, and they are woven together with the beams in a pattern older than memory. Rope of braided bark runs along the edges, smooth from many hands, and the railings are rounded and broad. There is no place here where you could slip, and no edge you need to think about. The house cradles you the same way the tree cradles every leaf. You can let your shoulders drop. You can let your jaw loosen. You can let your hands hang heavy at your sides.

Take a slow breath in, and let it go out slowly, and feel how the room breathes with you. The air up here is cool and clean and carries the scent of green bark and old rain. There is a trace of moss in it, and a trace of sweet resin, and very faintly something like warm bread, though no bread is baking. It is only the smell of the wood itself, which has been warmed all day by a kind sun and is now giving that warmth back to the dark.

This is a hidden place. The mist has seen to that, and the many leaves, and the long years of quiet. No path leads here that anyone could follow by accident. And that is why it feels so entirely yours tonight. You are not a visitor who must leave by morning. You are simply here, resting in the high green dark, in a house that was made for resting.

Let the sway carry you a little further into the room, and let your steps grow slow and soft, and let your eyes follow the warm glow that waits on the far side of the trunk.

## Chapter 2: Lantern, Mist, and Leaves

The glow comes from a single lantern, and it hangs from a curved iron hook beside the round window. It is a small lantern, the size of two cupped hands, with panes of thick amber glass set into a frame of dark old bronze. Inside, the flame is low and steady, a tiny golden tongue that leans a little when the house leans and straightens again when the house straightens. It makes a soft pool of light on the boards beneath it, and the edges of that pool melt slowly into the shadows, so that you cannot say where the light ends and the dark begins.

You walk toward it, slowly, in your socks or your bare feet, and the planks feel smooth and faintly warm. The light touches your hands first, and then your wrists, and then the sleeves of whatever soft thing you are wearing. It is the color of honey held up to a window. It makes everything it touches look a little more gentle than before. The grain of the wood shows in it like the grain of a slow river, and the bronze of the lantern glows a deep, quiet brown.

Beside the lantern is the window, and it is perfectly round. It is as wide as your open arms, set into the curved wall like a moon set into the sky, and its frame is carved with a thin ring of leaves, each leaf no bigger than your thumbnail. The glass is old and very slightly wavy, so the world on the other side seems to shimmer a little, as if seen through clear water. A wide wooden sill runs beneath it, deep enough to sit on, and worn smooth at the center by years of quiet elbows.

You lean on that sill, and you look out.

There is mist. It fills the whole forest below and around you, soft and pale and wide, and the light of the lantern lies on it like a gentle hand laid on a sleeping animal. Beyond the glass the mist is not white but silver, and in places a faint blue, and where the lantern reaches it turns a warm pearl color that seems to glow from within. It does not rush. It drifts. Little slow rivers of it wind between the trunks and slide over the highest branches, and the shapes of the leaves come out of it and go back into it, one after another, like thoughts that arrive and drift away without any need to be held.

The leaves nearest the window are broad and soft, each one rimmed with a thread of silver where the mist has settled on it. They hang quite still, and then a breath of air passes, and they all turn together, and the tiny drops on them gather the lantern light and glitter for a moment before they go dim. Beyond them are more leaves, and beyond those more still, layer after layer, each one a little paler and farther away, until the farthest ones are only a faint green dream in the grey.

Now listen. The leaves are whispering. It is not a loud sound. It is the softest sound a forest can make, a long, low hush that rises and falls like the edge of a slow tide. Each leaf says almost nothing, but ten thousand leaves together say a great and gentle word, over and over, and the word has no meaning except that all is well. You can hear it in front of you and to your left and far to your right, wide and soft and everywhere, a whisper that wraps around the treehouse like a shawl.

You stay at the window a while longer, with the lantern warm on your cheek and the mist moving slow beyond the glass. The whisper of the leaves goes on, and goes on, and each time it rises you feel a little more of the day loosen and lift away from your shoulders, the way the mist lifts from the forest as the night turns over.

When you are ready, and not before, you turn from the round window and look across the room toward the far wall, where a low shape waits in the shadows, soft and wide and inviting.

## Chapter 3: The Nest of Blankets

The low shape is your bed, and it is less a bed than a nest. It sits on a raised platform of smooth boards in the curve of the far wall, and the platform is hardly higher than your knee. Around its edge runs a low rim of woven willow, pale and supple, which keeps the blankets gathered in a gentle ring. Inside that ring is a deep, soft hollow, and the hollow is filled with wool.

Walk to it slowly. Each step is quiet, and the house sways a little with you, and the planks beneath your feet grow warmer the closer you come. This is the thing you notice most. The wood here is warm. All day the sun lay on the leaves above and on the boards of the roof, and the old timber drank in that light the way a stone drinks in the heat of a long afternoon. Now, in the dark, it gives the warmth back, slowly, evenly, with no hurry at all. The floor is warm. The wall at your shoulder is warm. When you rest the back of your hand against the bedpost, it is warm like the side of a sleeping animal, and you feel how kindly the tree has saved the day for you.

You kneel at the edge of the nest and let your fingers sink into the blankets. There are many of them, layered over each other in soft folds. The first is a thick wool the color of oatmeal, loosely woven, with a slight nap that tickles your palm. Under it lies a second, deeper blue-green, like the shade beneath a pine, and it is heavier and smoother. Under that is a quilt stuffed with something light as a cloud, and beneath the quilt is a wide, pale fleece that rises around you like a drift of snow that is somehow warm.

The wool smells of lanolin and a faint trace of smoke and a clean, dry sunlit air. You lift the oatmeal blanket to your face for a moment and breathe in, and it is the smell of a place where you were always meant to rest. It is soft against your cheek, and a little rough, in the way that good things are, and it holds the warmth of the wood below it like a gentle secret.

You sit on the edge of the nest for a while and let your hands rest in your lap. The blankets round you are warm now, and you feel them like a hand cupped around yours. The house rocks. The whisper of leaves continues, soft and constant. The lantern flame bows and rises.

And then, from very far away, from the depths of the misted forest, comes a single owl.

It calls once, low and round and mellow, a soft hooting note that rolls across the treetops like a stone dropped gently into a deep pool. It is far off, much farther than the nearest ridge of leaves, and the mist takes the edge from the sound and leaves only its warm center. It is a calm sound. It is the voice of the forest saying that the night is long and quiet and there is time. You listen, and the sound fades, ring after ring, until it is only a memory in the air.

For a while there is nothing but the leaves. And then, after a long, easy pause, the owl calls again, once, from the same far place, in the same unhurried tone. That is all. One distant owl, calling now and then across the dark. You do not need to look for it. You do not need to wonder where it sits. It is far away, and it is content, and its voice is part of the room now, like the lantern and the wool and the warm wood.

You feel your breath slow to meet the sound. The call is so low and so even that your body takes it in without effort, the way it takes in the warmth of a bath. Your shoulders settle another small measure. The muscles of your face soften. Even your hands, which were resting in your lap, grow heavier and more still.

The blankets wait. The wood is warm. The lantern is low. Outside, the mist moves in its silver rivers, and the leaves whisper their long word, and the whole high green house sways, and sways, and sways back.

It is time to lie down.

## Chapter 4: The Slow Breath of the Branches

You turn and ease yourself back into the nest, and the wool receives you. First your hips sink into the fleece, and then your shoulders find the quilt, and then your head settles into the lavender pillow with a small, soft sigh of linen. The blankets rise around you on both sides, and you draw the oatmeal wool up over your chest, and the blue-green wool over that, and the warmth gathers and holds. It is the exact warmth you needed, not too much and not too little, the warmth of being thoroughly looked after.

Your feet are covered. Your hands are covered. The cool air of the room touches only your face, lightly, like a friendly breath, and the rest of you lies wrapped in wool and quiet.

From here, you can see the high round ceiling, with its thin curved rafters meeting at the center like the ribs of a leaf. The little bundles of dried grass hang from them, and they sway, and in the glow of the lantern they cast slow, long shadows that drift back and forth across the boards. You watch them for a while without trying to watch. They move with the house. They are the same slow pace as everything else.

Now feel the sway more clearly, because lying down lets you feel it as it truly is. The whole nest moves, a little to the left and then a little to the right, in a long, gentle arc, and the arc takes a good many heartbeats to complete. It is like the rise and fall of a great chest in sleep. The tree breathes in, and the branches lean slowly one way, and you lean with them. The tree breathes out, and the branches return, and you return. In, and out. Lean, and return.

You do not need to do anything to join this rhythm. It carries you. Each time the nest leans, your body gives itself to the lean, and each time it comes back, you feel the faint pleasant weight of being lifted and set down again, lifted and set down, like a small boat on a wide, easy sea. There is nothing in the sway that is quick, and nothing that is uneven. It is the same soft motion, again and again, and with every repeat your body learns it a little more deeply, until you cannot tell your own breathing from the breathing of the branches.

Let your breath be slow. Let it follow the sway. As the nest leans, breathe in, long and quiet, and as the nest comes back, breathe out, slow and soft. There is no need to count. There is no need to measure. Only in, and out, and the warm wool rising and falling on your chest.

The lantern is lower now. Somewhere in the last while the flame has settled, as flames do when they are content, and it has shrunk to a small, round glow no bigger than a plum. The light on the boards has gone from honey to a deep amber, and the pool of gold beneath the lantern has grown narrow and quiet. The room is full of soft brown shadows. They are not dark in any heavy way. They are the shadows of a place that is simply at rest, the way a sleeping house rests with its lamps turned down.

Far away, the owl calls again, one low and mellow note. It reaches you as a soft round sound that rolls over the roof and settles in the wood, and you feel it, rather than hear it, in the warm quiet of the nest. After it, there is a long hush. The leaves breathe. The branches lean, and return.

Your arms grow heavy. Your legs grow heavy. The little muscles behind your eyes let go, one by one, as though each of them had been waiting all day for permission. The weight of your head sinks deeper into the pillow. Your jaw is loose, and your tongue is soft, and your forehead is smooth.

Everything you carried up here has been set down. It lies by the door with your slippers, and it can stay there until a much later time, when it will be light and small and easy to lift. For now it is night, and the forest is kind, and the nest is warm, and there is nothing at all to hold.

The sway goes on. The lantern glows. The leaves whisper, and the whispering grows slow, and slower, and soft.

## Chapter 5: Sleep in the Green Dark

The lantern is low now. It is only a small gold seed in the dark.

The branches lean. The branches return.

The mist is pale at the window. It moves without a sound.

The leaves speak very softly. They say that all is well.

The wool is warm on your chest. It rises with your breath, and it falls with your breath, and it is the lightest weight in the world.

The wood is warm beneath you. It gives back the day, slowly, kindly, without any hurry.

You are held. You are held by the tree, and by the house, and by the long green night.

The sway is slow. It is slower now. It is the breath of a very old and gentle friend, who is asleep beside you and wishes you the same.

In, and a long lean. Out, and a long return.

The forest is kind. It has always been kind. It will be kind in the morning, and in the evening after that, and in all the evenings that follow.

Far away, one owl calls, low and round. The sound drifts over the crowns, and over the mist, and over the whispering leaves, and it comes to rest somewhere in the wood, like a soft stone laid down in warm sand.

Then the quiet again. Warm quiet. Wide quiet.

Your hands are heavy. They are still.

Your breath is long. It is slow.

Your eyes are closed, and the lantern glows behind them, a small gold light.

The leaves whisper. The branches sway.

The wool is warm. The wood is warm.

You are safe. You are soft. You are asleep.

The mist drifts on.

The lantern burns low and gold.

The leaves are whispering, whispering, whispering.

And the treehouse rocks you, slowly, slowly, slowly, through the long green dark, and the forest holds you, and you sleep.
