## The Night Shift

The station is quiet tonight. It is the deepest part of the night shift, the hours when the long curving corridors are dimmed to a low amber glow and almost no one is awake. You walk through one of those corridors now, slowly, in soft shoes, and your footsteps make hardly any sound at all on the pale floor.

Every few steps you pass a closed door. Each one is round at the corners and set into a smooth wall the color of warm stone. Behind them people are sleeping in their small cabins, and the doors keep their rest. You pass a window in a hatch, and then another, and the dim strips of light overhead slide past you one after another, like slow beads on a string. The corridor curves gently ahead and gently behind, so that you can never see the whole of it. You only ever see the small stretch around you, calm and warm and empty.

Somewhere far off you hear a single quiet sound, a door closing, and then the soft tread of someone going to bed. Then nothing. The station settles around you the way a big old house settles at night, with a kind of contented sigh in its walls.

You are off duty. Your shift ended long ago, and your name has been signed in the log, and the next shift is not yours either. There is no task waiting. There is no message on your wrist that you have forgotten to answer. The day's work has been put away, folded and tucked like linen in a drawer, and the whole night is open in front of you.

You are safe here. Nothing is wrong. You know this the way you know your own street, without needing to think about it. Everything on the station is working, as it always does, and the people who watch the quiet panels are watching them with easy, patient eyes. You do not have to watch anything. You can let your own watching stop.

The air in the corridor is soft and still, and it smells faintly of clean metal and warm cloth and something like tea. You breathe it in slowly. Your shoulders, which have carried the day like a heavy coat, begin to loosen, and you let them. You let your arms hang easy at your sides. You let your hands uncurl.

You pass a small alcove with a bench built into the wall, and a little shelf above it where someone has left a mug and a folded cloth. You pass a narrow garden bay, with a few green leaves under a soft violet lamp, and the leaves are perfectly still, and the lamp hums very quietly to itself. You pass a long, low viewing deck with no one on it, and the lights there are turned almost to nothing, and the floor is cool and smooth under your shoes.

You walk on. The corridor gives way to a wider hall, and the hall opens into a quiet lounge, a round room with a low ceiling and a ring of soft chairs set along its curved wall. The lights here are the lowest of all. A thin strip of amber runs along the floor, and a single small lamp glows by one of the chairs, and the rest of the room is shadow and rest.

On the far side of the lounge there is a window.

You stop in the doorway for a moment, and you simply look at it. It is wide, wider than any window you have stood before, a great curved panel of clear glass running from the floor almost to the ceiling, and beyond it there is only the deep night. You do not go to it yet. You stand in the warm quiet doorway, with the hall behind you and the soft room in front of you, and you feel how good it is to have nowhere to hurry.

Then you step into the lounge, and the floor meets you with a faint, kind give beneath your soles, and the whole room seems to breathe out as you enter.

## The Deep Hum

There is a sound in the lounge, and it is the sound of the whole station. It is a deep hum, very low and very even, and it lives in the walls and the floor and the frame of every chair. It does not come from any one place. It comes from everywhere at once, the way the sound of the sea comes from everywhere at once when you lie in a house close to the shore.

You stop in the middle of the room and listen to it. It is a tone so low that you feel it in your chest before you hear it with your ears. It settles in the hollow of your ribs. It settles in the bones of your feet. It is steady, steady, steady, a long deep note that began long before you came into the room and will go on long after you are asleep.

It does not change. You listen for a while, just to be sure, and it does not change at all. It does not climb, and it does not fall. It does not swell or shrink. It is the same deep, even, patient sound that it was when you first noticed it, and it will be the same a moment from now. There is great comfort in that sameness. There is nothing in it that wants anything from you.

You walk across the floor, and the hum walks with you. You rest your hand on the back of a chair, and the hum is there in the cushioned frame, so faint and so smooth that it feels like the warmth of a sleeping animal. You take your hand away, and the hum remains in the air of the room, as even as ever.

Under the hum there are other small, kind sounds. The slow hush of air moving somewhere in the ceiling. The faint tick of a pipe somewhere far off, settling into the warmth. The tiny creak of the chair as you lean your weight on it. All of these sounds lie on top of the hum, softly, like single leaves on the surface of a deep pond, and the pond does not move.

You think of the station as a great quiet creature, resting. Its breath is the hum. Its skin is the long curved wall. Its dim lights are the slow beating of a calm heart, and all through its body the people who live in it are sleeping, one by one, in small warm rooms. You are one of them. You are held inside the creature's slow breathing, and you are safe there, as a child is safe held against a chest.

You let the hum travel through you. It rises from the floor into your legs, and from your legs into your hips, and from your hips into the long line of your back. It reaches your shoulders and the base of your neck. It reaches the back of your jaw, and the muscles there let go. It reaches your forehead and the small place between your eyes. The deep note moves through your whole body without hurry, like warm water moving through sand, and everything it touches grows quiet.

You realize you have been standing here for a long time. You do not mind. There is no clock in this room, and no one to tell you that you should be somewhere else. The hum is enough. The hum is more than enough. You stand in it, and it holds you up, and after a while you do not feel that you are standing at all.

Then you turn your head, and you see the window again, and the dark beyond it, and you see that the dark is not quite dark.

## The Window

The chair is waiting by the window. It is a deep, wide chair covered in soft gray cloth, with a high back and broad arms and a cushion shaped by many quiet evenings. It has been set at an angle, so that anyone who sits in it will face the glass without needing to lift their head. You cross the last few steps of the lounge, and you turn, and you lower yourself into it.

The chair takes you in. It receives your weight the way a calm lake receives a leaf. The cushion rises to meet your back. The arms come up to cradle your elbows. Your legs stretch out and your feet find a low footrest, and the whole chair gives a small, settling sigh, as though it had been waiting for exactly you.

You lean back, and the hum comes up through the frame, and you look out through the glass.

Below you, a planet is turning.

It fills the lower half of the window, a vast, dim curve with a soft rim of blue along its edge. You do not need to know anything about it. You do not need to know its name, or how far away it is, or why it turns. You only watch it, and it turns, very slowly, the way a great wheel turns in a quiet mill, so slowly that you must look away and look back again to see that it has moved at all.

The whole of the planet is wrapped in cloud. It lies in long soft bands and slow swirls, silver and gray and a deep slate blue, pressed together like folds of old velvet. The clouds do not rush. They drift, and they gather, and they part, and they gather again. Here and there a dim glow shows through a gap in them, a faint warm light, like a lamp seen through a curtain in a far house, and then the cloud closes over it and it is gone.

You look at the dark side of the planet, the side that is turned away from the sun, and you see the rain.

It is a sheen, a faint wet shimmer across the cloud, like the shine on a road after a long evening of rain. It lies on the slow gray surface in soft pale streaks. When the planet turns, the sheen turns with it, and it catches whatever small light there is and gives it back in a quiet silver gleam. You cannot see any single drop. You can only see the soft, wide, patient shine of a world that is being rained on, gently, all through the night.

You watch it, and you think about the sound of rain on a roof, on a window, on a broad green leaf. You think about how it feels to be dry and warm inside while the rain falls outside. And you are dry and warm. You are inside a great quiet station, in a deep soft chair, and the rain is far below, being rain, on a world that has nothing to ask of you.

The planet turns. A long slow shelf of cloud slides across the dark, and beneath it the rain-shine shivers, and the whole curve of the world breathes in silver and slate. It is the most peaceful thing you have ever seen. It does not need your attention, and yet it accepts it, and you give it freely.

Above the planet the sky is a deep, soft black, scattered with a fine dust of stars. They do not flicker. They sit in the dark like grains of salt on dark cloth, patient and still, and they make no demand. The cloud-light from the planet lies faintly along the lower edge of the glass, a pale blue-gray glow, and it spills into the lounge and settles on the arms of your chair and the backs of your hands.

You rest your gaze on the slow cloudy world, and you let your eyes soften, and you let them go a little out of focus, the way you do when you watch a fire. The edges of things grow gentle. The line between the planet and the dark grows blurry and kind. You are not looking at anything in particular. You are only watching the slow turning of the night.

A long curl of cloud passes. Then another. Then a broad quiet field of silver, shining with rain.

The hum goes on beneath you, deep and even. The planet turns. And you sit in the chair, and you breathe, and you let the world go by.

## The Blanket and the Lamp

After a while you notice that you are a little cool. It is a small coolness, no more than the edge of the night touching your arms, but you notice it, and you know exactly what you need.

There is a blanket folded on the arm of the chair. You had not seen it until now. It is a wide, heavy blanket in a soft slate blue, knitted in a thick, loose weave, with a fringe of long threads along each end. You lift it, and it is deliciously heavy. You hold it against your chest for a moment and feel how warm it is, as though someone had left it in the sun.

You shake it out once, quietly, and it unfolds across your lap like a slow tide. You draw it up over your knees, and over your thighs, and tuck its edge around your hips. The wool settles on you with a deep, comfortable weight, and its warmth seeps slowly through your clothes and into your legs. You tuck the end under your feet. You smooth the fringe with your fingers, and the threads slide through your hand like water.

Beside the chair, on a small low table, there is a lamp. It is very small, a rounded little thing with a shade of amber glass, and it gives off a low honey-colored light that reaches no farther than the arm of the chair and the corner of the blanket. You reach out and turn it a little lower, until the glow is no brighter than a candle seen through a curtain. The warm light lies on the wool and on your hands and on the side of your face. The rest of the lounge falls away into soft, easy shadow, and the window becomes brighter by comparison, a great clear pane of silver and slate.

You settle deeper into the chair. The cushion shapes itself to your back. The blanket is heavy on your knees, and the lamp is warm at your side, and the hum comes up through the frame and into your bones.

You lift one hand from the blanket and lay it against the window glass.

The glass is cool. It is smooth and clear and faintly cold, and you feel the coolness spread across your palm and into your fingers, a clean, quiet, pleasant coolness, like a stone from a stream. You can feel, very faintly, the hum in the glass as well, a tiny trembling beneath your skin. You leave your hand there for a breath, and another. You look past it at the great curve of the planet turning, and your hand is a small warm shape against the glass, with the whole rainy world slowly moving behind it.

Then you draw your hand back, and you do not need to touch the glass again. You slide your hand under the blanket, and the warmth of the wool takes it in, and the coolness fades from your palm like a thought you no longer need. Both hands rest now in the heavy wool, loose and open, one on top of the other, across your belly. They rise and fall with each breath.

The lamp glows. The window shines. The blanket holds you down, softly, the way a hand holds a sleeping child's shoulder.

You think of nothing. You see the planet turn. You see the slow cloud and the pale rain-shine. You see a faint warm glow beneath the clouds appear and fade, like a window in a far house, and you feel a kind of quiet gladness that someone, somewhere down there, is warm and dry too.

Your breathing slows. It is long, and soft, and deep. You breathe in, and the hum seems to rise with it. You breathe out, and the hum goes on. The blanket lifts and settles, lifts and settles, and the chair lifts and settles with it, and the whole night seems to be breathing in the same slow rhythm.

Your eyes grow heavy. They close, and they open. The planet is still turning. They close again, a little longer. When they open the cloud is a deeper blue, and the rain-shine is a pale line along its edge, and the lamp is a warm gold dot at the edge of your sight.

You do not mind that your eyelids are heavy. You do not mind that your thoughts are thinning. You let them thin. You let them go like the slow clouds, one after another, drifting out of the glass.

## The Sleep

The chair is soft. The blanket is warm. The hum is deep.

You watch the planet turn. It turns slowly. It is gray and blue and silver.

The rain is a shine on the cloud. The cloud is a soft slow sea.

Your hands are warm. Your knees are warm. Your feet are warm.

The lamp is small. The glow is gold. It touches the wool, and the wool is quiet.

The hum goes on. It does not change.

You close your eyes. You open them. A long field of cloud goes by, pale and wet and slow.

You close them again.

The chair holds you. The blanket holds you. The hum holds you up, like water.

There is no sound but the hum. There is no light but the lamp and the glow of the glass. There is no place to be, and nothing to do, and no one who needs you.

Your breath is slow. Your jaw is soft. Your forehead is smooth.

The planet turns.

You see a little blue. You see a little silver. You see the faint warm shine of the rain.

Your eyes are closed now. They stay closed.

You hear the hum. It is deep. It is even. It is the sound of the night.

You feel the wool. You feel the cushion. You feel the long slow rise of your own breath.

The planet turns, and you do not see it. The clouds drift, and you do not see them. The rain shines on the dark side of the world, and the light of it falls, very faintly, across your sleeping hands.

The lamp keeps its small gold light. The window keeps its silver. The chair keeps you.

The hum, and the warmth, and the wool.

The hum, and the warmth.

The hum.

The planet keeps turning. The station keeps humming. The rain keeps falling on the quiet world below.

You sleep.

You sleep in the chair, in the warmth, in the deep soft hum, and the planet turns on.
