## The Shop Behind the Closed Door

It is night, and you are inside.

The door is shut behind you, and the bolt has been slid across with a small, dull sound that you felt more than heard. The shop is closed. The shutters on the front window are drawn. The sign on the hook outside has been turned to its sleeping side, and nobody will come knocking now. The whole street has gone quiet, and the quiet has been left on the other side of the wood, where it belongs.

You are staying the night. That was decided a long while ago, quietly, in the way that good things are sometimes decided, with a nod and a kettle and no need for anyone to explain. The old alchemist who keeps this shop has left a place for you. You are expected here. You are wanted here.
So there is nothing you need to do. Your boots are by the door, where they have already begun to dry. Your coat hangs on a peg beside a leather apron and a fringed shawl, and the three of them lean together in the dim light like old friends. You are safe. You are already safe. You were safe from the moment you crossed the threshold, and the house has simply been waiting for you to notice.

Take a slow breath in. The air in here is thick and soft, and it carries many layers. There is the warmth of old wood. There is the faint sweetness of beeswax. There is dust, but the kind of dust that is gentle, the kind that has settled onto things that are loved and left alone. And underneath all of it, there are herbs, so many herbs, dried and hung and kept for years, giving up their scent one slow breath at a time.

Let the breath go out. Let your shoulders go with it.

You look around the shop, slowly, with no hurry at all, because there is nowhere else to be.

The shelves climb the walls from the floor almost to the rafters. They are dark wood, worn smooth at the edges by generations of careful hands. On them stand jars, hundreds of jars, in every shape that a jar can take. Some are tall and narrow, with long necks. Some are round and squat, like sleeping toads. Some are the color of honey, some the color of moss, and some are the clear, pale blue of a winter morning. Each one has a cork or a wax seal, and each one has a little paper label tied on with thread. The handwriting is small and looping and old, and you do not try to read it.

The lamplight moves across the glass. It is a low lamp, set on the counter, with a shade of amber glass, and it throws a pool of honey-colored light that reaches the first two shelves and then fades gently into the dark above. In that pool the jars glow softly from within, as if each one held a small, patient ember. Higher up, beyond the light, they are only shapes. Rounded shoulders. Pale corks. A gleam where the glass catches a little warmth.

Between the shelves, from hooks and nails and long wooden poles, hang the dried bundles. There are so many of them. Sprigs tied with string, bunches bound at the stem and hung upside down, long garlands of leaves and little blossoms drooping from the beams. They hang very still. Their shadows hang beneath them, stretched long and soft on the plaster wall, and when the fire moves, the shadows sway just slightly, as if the bundles were breathing in their sleep.

Some are gray-green. Some are the faded purple of an evening sky. The ceiling is low, and so the bundles seem to form a second ceiling, a soft and fragrant one, a roof of dried summer.

On the counter there is a brass scale, resting level and quiet, and a ledger closed with a ribbon, and a small bell that nobody will ring tonight.

And at the back of the shop, past the counter and past the last tall shelf, there is a glow.

It is small, and low, and warm. It is a different light from the lamp, deeper, more orange, with a slow pulse in it, like a heartbeat that has learned to be gentle. You know what it is before you reach it. You can hear it, very softly, from here. A tiny, dry, crackling whisper. The sound of wood giving up its sunlight.

You begin to walk toward it, one easy step at a time, with the whole sleeping shop around you, and every jar on every shelf keeping watch over your quiet night.

## A Small Iron Stove

The stove sits in an alcove at the back of the shop, set into a nook of old brick that has been darkened by many winters. It is not large. It is a small, round-bellied iron stove, black and a little scuffed, standing on four short feet, with a flat top worn smooth and a narrow pipe that rises into the wall and carries the smoke away into the night. There is a little door at the front with a latch of bent iron, and a mica window in the door, and through that window the fire glows.

You stop a few steps away and simply look.

The flames are low. They are not tall or hungry. They are small, slow flames, curling lazily over a bed of glowing coals, the color of honey at the tips and the deeper color of amber at the root. Every so often a log shifts, and a little shower of sparks lifts and settles, and the light on the brick behind the stove brightens and then softens again, like a slow, easy breath.

The warmth reaches you before you reach it. It touches your face first, a gentle hand against your cheeks. It touches your hands. You feel it on the front of your coat, then on your knees, then on the soles of your feet, where the cold from outside is still hiding, and you feel that cold begin to loosen and melt away, a little at a time, thread by thread.

This is real warmth. It is not a trick of the mind. It is the old, honest warmth of iron and wood and flame, the warmth that people have gathered around since before there were words for it. It rests on your skin like a folded blanket, and it has been filling the alcove all evening, long before you came, so that the room was ready for you.

You step closer and lower yourself down onto the low bench that stands beside the stove.

The bench is wooden, long enough for two, and the seat has been worn into a gentle hollow by all the people who have sat here before you, listening to the same fire. The wood beneath you is warm already, and it gives its heat back to you now, into your back and your legs.

Now you listen.

The crackle is soft. It is not the sharp snapping of a fire that is too hot or too new. It is the quiet, patient crackle of a fire that has settled in for the night. A small tick. A pause. A low, papery whisper, like someone turning a page in another room. A soft pop, far down in the coals, and then nothing, and then a gentle hiss as a bit of sap sighs out of a log and is gone.

You do not need to follow it. The crackle will come when it comes, and the warm silence between the crackles is just as good.

The firelight is honey-colored. It lies across the floor in a long, soft shape that wavers a little at its edges. It climbs the nearest shelf, where it catches in the glass of three small jars and turns them into three small lanterns, and it moves slowly over the hanging bundles, so that the purple of the lavender glows, and dims, and glows again.

On the hearthstone at your feet there is a basket of split logs, stacked neatly, the bark still on. Nothing here needs tending tonight. The fire will keep itself, slowly, and there is nothing to be fed or watched or minded. You are only here to be warm.

Your hands open in your lap. Your fingers uncurl, one by one, in the heat. You did not know they were still holding on to the day, but they are letting go now, and you feel it, and it is a good feeling, like putting down a basket you have carried a long way.

The stove ticks as the iron warms and settles. It makes a small metallic sound, almost musical, a single soft note, and then it is silent again. The coals pulse. The light breathes. And you sit, and you are warm, and the warmth goes on.

## The Copper Cauldron on the Low Fire

There is something else on the stove.

You notice it now, because your eyes have grown used to the glow. On the flat iron top, set over the place where the fire burns lowest and steadiest, stands a small copper cauldron. It is round and old, the color of a polished penny at the belly and darker, almost brown, around the rim. It has two little ring handles and a lid that sits slightly crooked, and a thread of pale steam leaks out from beneath the lid, rising in a slow, lazy curl.

It does not boil. It does not bubble or spit or roll. This cauldron is on the lowest of low fires, and it only keeps itself warm, the way a sleeping animal keeps itself warm, deep and quiet. Somewhere inside it, there is a soft sound.

Blip.

A single, round little sound, like a drop of water falling into a still pond. And then a pause. A long, slow pause, in which the fire ticks and the steam curls and nothing at all happens.

Blip.

And a small, low sigh, as the lid lifts a hair and settles again.

That is all it does. It blips, and it sighs, and it is quiet. You listen to it for a while, and you find that the rhythm is slower than your own breath, and your breath begins to follow it without being asked, in a little and out a little, softer and slower, until there is no difference between the two.

Blip.

Sigh.

The steam that escapes is faint and warm and pale. It rises in a thin ribbon, wavers in the heat above the stove, and spreads into the air of the alcove, and you can smell it. It is a soft, herbal smell, green and sweet and a little like honey. It does not tell you what is inside. It does not need to. It is only a smell, a gentle one, and it mixes with the other smells of the room until you cannot tell which is which.

Because the room is full of lavender.

You noticed it when you came in, but now it is stronger, drawn out by the warmth. The dried bundles near the stove are letting go of their scent, slowly, generously, as they have done every night for months. Lavender is a soft, powdery, faintly sweet smell, like a clean pillowcase folded away in a cedar chest. It drifts down from the beams and settles over your shoulders.

And with it comes rosemary. It is greener and sharper than the lavender, but not harsh, only clear, like a cool hand on a warm forehead. It smells of hillsides and old stone walls and rain that has not quite arrived. The rosemary and the lavender weave together in the warm air, one over the other, and between them there is the faintest trace of woodsmoke, and beeswax, and the dry, golden smell of old paper.

You breathe it all in, slowly. You do not need to name each scent. You only let them come and go, one by one, like quiet guests passing through a room.

Blip.

The cauldron has no one tending it. That is the best part. Nobody needs it to be anything other than what it is. It is simply there, being warm, being quiet, making its small round sounds in the dark. It is part of the room's breathing.

You watch the thread of steam as it climbs. It catches the firelight and turns pale gold, and then it thins and vanishes somewhere near the beams. Another thread follows it. They rise and vanish, so slow that your eyes begin to soften, and the edges of everything blur a little, in the warm way that things blur when you are very comfortable.

The copper gleams, a bright, thin line of orange on the shoulder, and the reflection trembles very slightly with each blip.

Blip.

The sound drifts around the room, to the counter, to the farthest shelves, where it is very faint. The walls are thick, and the beams are old, and the sound stays inside, with you, in the warm.

And you do not drink anything. There is nothing you need to take. The cauldron is not for you to touch, or to taste, or to think about. It is a sound, only a sound, and a faint herbal warmth in the air, and a thread of steam that rises and is gone. It is a quiet companion by the fire, like a sleeping cat on a windowsill, and you are content to sit near it and listen.

Sigh.

The lid settles. The fire ticks. The lavender drifts down.

You sit, and the cauldron blips, and the night goes on being gentle.

## Wool, a Cushion, and the Dark Street

Beside you on the bench there is a blanket.

It was there all along, folded in a neat square at the end of the seat, and you only see it now. It is wool, thick and soft, woven in wide stripes of oatmeal and deep green and a faded rust-red, with a fringe along each end that has been knotted by hand. It smells of the stove, a little, and of cedar, and of clean air that has dried in the wind. You lift a corner of it and it is heavier than you expected, in the best way, with the comfortable weight of something that has kept many people warm.

You unfold it and draw it over your knees.

At once the warmth gathers. The wool holds it close, around your legs, then your lap, then up around your waist, and you pull it higher, over your shoulders, and tuck the edge beneath your chin. It is a little rough against your fingers and soft against your skin, and the warmth inside it is your own warmth, returned to you, kept safe. You settle down into it like a small animal settling into a nest.

There is a cushion too, at the far end of the bench, plump and square, covered in faded embroidered cloth. Small flowers have been stitched across it in dull gold thread. You draw it toward you, and set it against the arm of the bench, and lean into it. It gives beneath your weight with a soft sigh of feathers, and it holds your head and your shoulder exactly as a cushion should.

Now you are low, and close to the stove. You can feel the heat on your cheek, a gentle glow, no more than you want. The firelight lies across the blanket in warm bands, and the colors of the wool deepen in it, green into near-black, rust into ember. Your hands are folded beneath the fringe. Your feet are stretched toward the hearthstone, and the soles of them are warm, completely warm, for the first time since morning.

You can hear the room around you now, in its small, steady way. The crackle of the logs. The blip of the cauldron. And beneath those sounds, the tiny, whispering noises of an old building at night, a beam easing, a pipe settling, the faint tick of the ceiling as the warm air rises into the dark. They are simply the house breathing, as every old house does when it has been warmed and left in peace.

The shutters on the front window are closed, but through the gaps between the slats there is a faint thread of dark blue. That is the street. It lies out there beyond the glass, quiet and unlit, with its cobblestones glistening a little, and its rooftops folding into the night. A lamp burns somewhere at the far corner, small and yellow. A little wind moves along the gutters, and then stops. Nobody walks there. Nothing happens there at all.

And you find that you do not mind the street. It is not important. It is a dark, still place where the evening has already ended, and it does not need you, and you do not need it. It is only the outside of this warm room, the cold shell around a soft center, and you are at the center, wrapped in wool, in the glow.

The warm room is a secret kept by the dark, and the dark keeps it well.

The air is drowsy now. It has the heavy, golden quality that air has near a fire late at night, when the light is low and every sound has lost its edge. The jars on the nearest shelf glow quietly. The ledger lies closed. The bell hangs still.

Your breath is slow. It has been slow for a while now, and you have only just noticed. In, and a pause. Out, and a longer pause. The wool rises and falls over your chest, gently, as though it were breathing with you.

The fire ticks.

The cauldron blips.

You tuck your chin a little deeper into the blanket, and your cheek finds the cushion, and the small gold flowers press softly against your skin. The embroidery is a little rough, and a little warm, and it smells of lavender, because everything here smells of lavender, and rosemary, and the slow sweetness of old wood.

Your eyelids are heavy. They have been heavy for some time. They do not need to be held open anymore. The fire is still there, even when you close your eyes, a warm orange glow behind your eyelids, soft and slow and breathing.

You let them close, just for a moment, and the warmth stays.

## Sleep by the Fire

The fire is low and gold.

The wool is warm. The bench is warm. Your hands are warm.

The cauldron blips.

You hear it, and it is a little farther away than it was. Not gone. Only farther. Like a sound in the next room. Like a sound from the far end of a long hallway, where a door stands open onto a quiet place.

Blip.

The crackle is a whisper. A tick. A pause.

Lavender, soft and slow. Rosemary, faint and green.

Your breath is long.

Your body is heavy, and the heaviness is kind. It sinks into the bench. It sinks into the wool. It sinks into the warm gold of the room, and the room holds it, as the room has held everything, every night, for years.

The shelves are dim. The jars are dim. The bundles sway, once, and are still.

Blip.

Farther now.

The street is dark. It is very far away. It is not important.

The stove is warm. The glow is steady. The shadows lie soft across the floor and do not move.

You are safe. You are warm. You are here.

Sigh.

The sound is small. It is small, and slow, and gentle.

Your thoughts grow quiet. They drift, like steam, like thin threads of steam rising from a lid, rising into the beams, rising into the dark, and fading.

The wool is soft.

The fire is low.

Blip.

A long, long pause.

The lavender drifts down.

Warm. Soft. Slow.

The coals glow. The light breathes. The night is deep and kind.

Far away, a small round sound. Far, and fainter.

Blip.

And the fire whispers, and the wool holds you, and the shop sleeps around you, with every jar in its place and every bundle in its shadow.

You sleep.

Warm.

Slow.

Sleeping by the fire.
