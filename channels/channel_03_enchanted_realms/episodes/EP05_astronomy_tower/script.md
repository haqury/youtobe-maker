## The Top of the Tower

The night is already quiet when this story begins, and you are already exactly where you want to be. You are sitting at the very top of the astronomy tower of the old academy, wrapped in a thick wool blanket, and nothing at all is asked of you here. The lessons ended hours ago. The long corridors below have emptied one by one, the heavy doors have been closed, the candles in the tall windows have been pinched out, and the whole great building has settled into itself like a large animal asleep in a field. You are above all of it now, and you are warm.

Let your body know that. Let your shoulders know it first. They have carried the whole day, and they can put it down on the stone behind you, where the day will stay until morning. Let your arms grow heavy inside the blanket. The wool is dense and a little rough at the edges, the kind of wool that has been washed many times and has only become softer for it. It is folded over your knees, tucked under your feet, and gathered up around your neck, so that only your face is left out in the open night. The air on your face is cool and clean and gentle, and it makes everything under the blanket feel even warmer by comparison.

There is no class tonight. There is no chart to copy and no page to finish. Nobody is waiting for you to say anything or to remember anything. The tower is simply a quiet place that happens to be high, and you have come up here only to rest in it. Behind you, a narrow stair turns down into the dark, a long spiral of worn steps that you climbed slowly, one at a time, with your hand on the cool curve of the wall. You are not thinking about the stair now. It is far below, and it has done its work. You are here.

Feel the floor beneath you. It is made of wide, flat stones, fitted closely together, smoothed by more years of footsteps than anyone has counted. Even through the folded blanket you are sitting on, you can feel how solid it is. The stone does not shift. It does not creak. It holds you the way the ground holds a mountain, without effort and without any wish to be thanked. Your weight settles down into it, a little more with each breath, and the stone takes that weight and asks for nothing.

Now listen to the wind. It is a soft wind, a slow and steady one, and it moves across the top of the tower the way a hand moves across the surface of still water. It does not push. It does not whistle. It only passes, and as it passes it makes a low and even sound, a long soft breath that rises a little and falls away and rises again. Somewhere out along the roofs it brushes the old slate tiles. Somewhere it moves through the tops of the dark trees beyond the academy walls, and the trees answer with a faint leafy rustle, like a crowd of people whispering in another room.

You can hear that whole wide whisper from where you sit, and none of it is meant for you to follow. It is only there, the way the night is there. You do not have to listen closely. You can let the sound arrive and let it go, arrive and go, the same unhurried rhythm as the rise and fall of your own chest.

Around you, the tower top is a wide round platform with a low wall of stone running along its edge. Nothing here needs your attention. The wall is there, the wind is there, the stone is there, and the blanket is there, all of them doing their small, steady jobs so that you can do nothing.

So you do nothing. You sit, and you breathe, and the wool holds the warmth of your body close. High above the sleeping academy, in the cool and gentle dark, you are already safe, already comfortable, already a little more tired than you were a moment ago. There is nowhere to go. There is only the tower, and the night, and the long slow breath of the wind going by.

## The Copper Telescope

A few steps from where you sit, at the center of the platform, stands the telescope. You have known it for a long time, in the easy way that you know the furniture of a room you love. It rests on a sturdy wooden stand with three thick legs, and the long tube of it is made of copper, warm brown in the faint light, gone soft and dark in places where many hands have held it over many years. There are small green patches along the brass rings where the metal has aged, and they look like moss on an old garden wall. A few narrow bands of polished metal still catch what little light there is and hold it, a thin glow that runs along the length of the tube like a quiet thought.

You do not need to do anything with the telescope at all. But you feel like moving a little, in the slow way a cat moves when it decides to change windows, and so you keep the blanket wrapped close and rise from the stone. The blanket comes with you, a warm heavy cloak, trailing a little behind your feet. Three slow steps, and you are beside the stand.

Rest your hand on the tube. The copper is cool, but not cold, and it has a faint smoothness, like the inside of a shell. The metal has a kind of patience in it. It has stood in this spot through many nights, pointed at the same wide sky, and it has never once been in a hurry.

At the narrow end of the tube there is a small round opening where an eye can rest. Its rim is polished, and it is edged with a thin circle of soft leather, worn smooth and dark by all the faces that have leaned against it. Lean in now, slowly. You do not have to press. You only have to bring your eye close, and rest it there, the way you would rest your forehead against a cool window on a long and quiet journey.

For a moment there is only a faint round darkness, soft as velvet. Your eye relaxes into it. Then, little by little, the darkness begins to fill. It does not fill all at once. It fills the way a pond fills with the first small reflections of evening, one pale point and then another, until the whole round window holds a gentle scatter of stars.

They are pale stars, soft and silver and quiet. They look like grains of fine salt spilled across black cloth, or like the faint shine on the surface of dark water far down a river. Some are so small that you only see them when you stop trying to see them, and they fade in and out at the edge of your gaze like slow breathing. You do not need to name any of them. You are only looking, the way you look at snow falling outside a window, without any wish to keep it.

Now the stars begin to drift. Very slowly, so slowly that you could not say when it started, the whole pale scatter moves across the round window of the telescope, gliding from one edge toward the other. It is the gentlest motion you can imagine. It is like a slow river carrying a thousand small lamps, and you are standing on the bank, watching them go by, with no need to follow. Some drift in from the dark on one side, and some slip quietly out on the other, and the river never empties and never hurries. There is always another small pale light, coming up out of the dark, passing across, and fading softly away.

Your eye grows heavy as you watch. The lid wants to close, and you let it close partway, so that the stars blur into soft round glows, each one wearing a faint halo like a candle seen through a foggy pane. Nothing about this needs effort. You are only a quiet person resting an eye against a copper tube, watching a slow river of pale stars go by.

The wind moves around you in its easy way, lifting a loose corner of the blanket and laying it gently down again. After a while, when your neck feels warm and loose and your eye is soft, you draw back from the telescope, as gently as you came, and the whole slow river stays where it was, going on without you, perfectly content.

## Stone, Wool and Lantern

You turn away from the telescope and walk slowly toward the edge of the platform, where the low stone wall runs in its long curve around the top of the tower. This is the parapet, and it has been here for longer than the telescope, longer than the stair. The stones of it are large and pale and rounded at the top by weather, joined by thin lines of old mortar that has gone soft and friendly with age.

Stop in front of it. Rest your hands on the top of the wall, so that only your fingertips touch the stone. It is cool and slightly gritty, and it feels exactly as stone ought to feel. A thousand evenings have leaned on this wall, and not one of them has made it any less strong.

Now find the place where the wall is wide enough to sit on, a smooth broad stretch near the corner, and lower yourself down against it. Your back finds the stone, and it is cool, but the blanket is thick between you and the cold, and the wool holds your warmth in the way a good friend holds a long silence, without any hurry to fill it. You tuck the blanket under your legs. You draw it up again around your shoulders. You let your head tip back until it rests against the stone, and you feel the whole weight of your body sink in.

The wind finds you here. It comes over the top of the wall, soft and steady, and it moves along the outside of the blanket, and you can feel it on the wool in the gentlest way. It does not get through. It only brushes over the surface, and the thick weave shivers a little, a faint small ripple of fibers, like grass moving on a hillside. Listen to the sound of it, a quiet dry whisper against the wool, almost too soft to hear. It is the sound of the night checking that you are warm, and finding that you are, and moving on.

Beside your feet there is a small lantern. You set it there earlier, a plain little lantern with a frame of dark iron and four panes of thick, slightly clouded glass, and a short, fat candle burning inside. You placed it in the corner where the parapet meets the next wall, in a small sheltered nook, so that the wind passes over it and never reaches the flame. The flame stands perfectly upright. It is a small, gold, patient thing, no taller than your thumb, and it does not flicker or lean. It only glows, a steady warm point inside its cloudy glass.

Look at it for a while. The light from it is soft and low and honey-colored, and it spills a small round pool across the stones around your feet. In that pool the stone looks warm, almost like bread crust, and the edge of the blanket glows a deep gentle brown. Tiny shadows rest in the cracks between the stones, and the iron frame of the lantern throws four thin dark lines onto the floor, trembling very slightly, the way the lines in a calm lake tremble when a leaf falls on them. The whole little circle of light feels like a room inside the larger room of the night, and you are sitting inside it, kept and sheltered.

The lantern does not light the sky, and it does not need to. It lights only what is near, your feet, your hands, the corner of the wool, the old stone. Beyond its small glow, the night is wide and dark and calm, and that is good too. The small warm light and the large cool dark sit side by side as they have always done.

Feel how the warmth moves through you now. It begins in your feet, where the wool is thickest, and rises slowly through your legs. It spreads across your lap and up your chest, and it settles in your arms like a held breath being let go.

The wind goes over the wool. The flame stands in its glass. The stone holds your back. You are a small, quiet shape at the edge of a tower, in the gentle company of a lantern and a blanket and the long soft night, and everything about this place has agreed to keep you comfortable until morning.

## The Wide Calm Sky

Now you let yourself lean back. You have been sitting upright against the parapet, and there is no more need to hold yourself up. You slide down a little, slowly, until your shoulders rest on the broad flat top of the wall and the back of your head is cradled in the folded edge of the blanket. Your legs stretch out along the stones. The lantern glows at the side of your vision, a small warm friend. And above you, all the way up, the sky opens out.

It is wide. It is wider than you remembered, a vast quiet dome of deep blue and black that begins at the edge of the tower and goes on without end. There is no ceiling and no roof, and no wall to hold it in. There is only the long soft dark, scattered with pale stars, resting over the whole sleeping world like a dark cloth laid gently over a table. You feel very small beneath it, and that smallness is a comfort, the way it is a comfort to be a single quiet child in a house that is full of sleeping family.

Let your eyes rest on the sky without looking for anything. You do not need to find a pattern. You do not need to trace a line from one star to another. Let the pale lights come and go at the edges of your sight, a gentle glitter near the top of the sky, a thin soft haze across the middle, like breath on a cold window. Some of the stars are steady and some seem to swim a little, and all of them are calm. The sky has the look of a lake at night, perfectly still, holding every small light and keeping all of it safe.

Feel the tower beneath you. You might think that a tower this high would move, that it would sway or tremble in the wind, but it does not. It stands as steady as a great old tree with deep roots. The wind passes around it and over it, and the stones simply stay where they are. When you rest your palm flat on the wall, you can feel the deep calm of it, a stillness that goes all the way down.

You are held, by the wool and the stone, by the tower and the long ground beneath the tower.

The wind is slower now, or perhaps it only seems that way because you are slower. It touches the top of the blanket, brushes the edge of your hair, and drifts away across the roofs. It has a long, easy sound, like a very far-off sea, the sound of something large and kind breathing in its sleep. You let your own breath follow it, in and out, in and out, long and unhurried, and the two breaths find the same slow pace and move together.

Your eyelids are heavy. They close, and then open a little, and the stars are still there, softer now, a little blurred, each one a small pale bloom of light. You let them close again. Behind your closed eyes the stars are still drifting, very slowly, the way they drifted in the round window of the telescope, a slow silver river with no beginning and no end. You can feel the cool of the night on your cheek and the warmth of the wool at your neck, and the two things sit together easily, like two gentle voices speaking in another room.

There is nothing to remember. There is nothing to decide. The sky is wide and calm above you, and the tower is steady beneath you, and the lantern burns in its little glass, and you are exactly where you should be. Let your whole body grow heavy. Let your jaw loosen. Let your hands lie open under the wool. The night is long, and kind, and it asks for nothing at all.

## The Wind Becomes a Hush

The stars drift. The wind goes by.

You are warm in the wool. Your breath is slow. Your hands are soft and still, and the blanket is heavy on your chest, a comfortable weight, the weight of a quiet thing that has made up its mind to stay.

The lantern glows by your feet. Its small gold flame does not move. It lights the stone and the edge of the wool, and nothing else. The rest of the night is wide and dark and gentle, and it needs no light from you.

Somewhere far below, the academy sleeps. The corridors are empty and the great doors are closed. The candles are out. The long stair is dark and the old steps are quiet. All the sleeping rooms hold their sleeping people, one by one, like small lamps held in the palm of the dark. You are one of them. You are only a little higher than the rest.

The tower is steady. The stone is cool and still. It holds you the way it has held every quiet night, and it will hold this one, too.

The wind is softer now. It was a breath, and now it is less than a breath. It moves over the wall and over the wool, and it makes a long low sound, a sound with no edges. It is a hush. It is the sound of a very large room being very quiet. It passes over your face and over your hair, and it leaves you more still than it found you.

The pale stars are far above. They are soft and slow. They drift, and drift, and you do not follow them. You only let them go by, as you let the wind go by, as you let the day go by. There is nothing to hold. There is nothing to keep.

Your eyes are closed. Your thoughts are slow and small, like the last few stars at the edge of a window. One drifts. Then another. Then they are very far apart.

The wool is warm. The stone is calm. The lantern is a small gold point, and then a soft glow, and then only a color behind your eyes.

Your breath is long. Your body is heavy. The tower stands in the dark, and the night is kind.

The wind is a hush. The hush is a sleep.

Rest now. Rest, under the pale stars, in the warm wool, high above the quiet academy.

The wind is a hush.

Rest.
