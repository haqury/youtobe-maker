## Chapter 1: The Journey Is Over

The journey is already over. You have come all the way up the mountain road, and the road has ended here, at the door of a wooden tavern, and you have stepped through that door into warmth. There is nothing left to walk and nothing left to carry. You are in a quiet room on the second floor, and the long day has folded itself away behind you like a map put back in a drawer.

The tavern is old, and it is built entirely of timber. The beams are thick and dark, cut from tall mountain pines, and they have been warmed by many winters of firelight until they glow faintly brown, the colour of tea. The walls are made of broad planks, fitted close, and the floor beneath your feet is made of the same wood, worn smooth in the places where many travellers have walked before you. Everything here has been touched by kind hands for a very long time.

Your room is small, and it is exactly the right size. It has a low ceiling with a single crossbeam, a narrow wooden door, one small window, and a bed. That is all, and it is enough. The corners are soft with shadow. The air smells of pine and woodsmoke and clean linen, and it is warm enough that you can feel your shoulders letting go.

Behind you, the door is closed, and the latch has been dropped from the inside. It is an iron latch, simple and heavy, set into a plain wooden bracket. You lowered it yourself, gently, with one hand. You did it because you chose rest, the way you might close a book at the end of a long chapter. There is nothing outside that door that needs keeping out. The corridor beyond is quiet, and the stairs are quiet, and the whole house is settling into its evening. The latch is only a small, pleasant sound, the sound of a day being finished.

Stand for a moment in the middle of the room, and feel the floor hold you. The boards are warm beneath your socks. They give very slightly, the way old floors do, and then they are firm again. You can feel the whole tavern around you, a great sleeping animal made of wood, breathing slowly in the cold mountain night. You are inside its warmth, and it has taken you in.

You are completely safe here. The walls are thick. The roof is steep and heavy, built to carry the winter snow without complaint. The door is shut, the window is shut, and the mountain outside is quiet. There is no road to watch and no hour to keep. The only thing left to do tonight is to rest, and everything in the room has been arranged to help you do it.

Let your shoulders fall. Let your arms hang loose. Let your jaw soften, and your tongue lie quietly in your mouth. Breathe in the smell of pine and linen, and breathe out the last of the road. You have arrived. You have been arriving all day, and now the arriving is done.

The room is lit by a single small lamp on a wooden shelf beside the bed. It is an oil lamp with a round glass, and the flame inside it is low and steady and the colour of butter. The light does not reach every corner. It gathers on the shelf and on the edge of the quilt and on a patch of the plank wall, and everything it touches looks gentle and close. The dark beyond it is not a dark that hides anything. It is only the soft edge of the room, where the light has grown tired and gone to sleep.

Take a slow breath, and feel how still the room is. Nothing in it is waiting for you. Nothing is expected. The tavern has done all its evening work, and the long hush of the night has begun. You are a guest here, a tired and welcome guest, and all the rest of the evening belongs to you.

## Chapter 2: A Warm Bed Under a Heavy Quilt

Look at the bed, because the bed is the heart of this room. It is a wide wooden frame, made of the same dark pine as the beams, with a high headboard carved in a simple pattern of leaves. The posts are smooth from years of hands. The mattress is thick and stuffed with wool and straw, and it sits a little high, so that when you sit on the edge your feet just touch the floor.

On the bed lies a quilt, and the quilt is heavy. It is made of many squares of cloth sewn together, in soft reds and browns and deep forest greens, and each square has been stitched by hand with small even stitches. Beneath the patchwork there is thick wool filling, and the whole thing has a weight that you can see before you touch it, the way a pile of snow has a weight. When you rest a hand on it, your hand sinks a little, and the quilt rises softly around your fingers.

Under the quilt lies clean linen. The sheets are pale and cool and crisp, and they smell of fresh air and of a line in the wind, and a faint trace of lavender. The pillow is full and round, with a white cover, and there is a smaller pillow beside it. The linen has been turned back at one corner, just enough to show you where to slide in. It is a small kindness, and you feel it as a kind of welcome.

Sit down on the edge of the bed for a moment, and let your hands rest on your knees. The mattress takes your weight with a soft whisper of straw. It is not quite silent, and the faint sound is lovely, the sound of a good bed saying that it is ready. Look down at your boots. They are still on your feet, and they have carried you a long way.

Unlace them slowly. The laces are stiff from the cold, and your fingers are slow, and that is fine. There is no hurry. Loosen the first boot and ease it off, and feel the warm air meet your foot through the sock. Set it down beside the bed. Then the second boot, the same way, loosened and eased and set down beside the first. Two boots, side by side on the planks, resting at last.

Your feet feel strange and light without them, and then they feel wonderful. You curl your toes in the wool, and the warmth of the room rises through the floor and into your ankles. Your soles are tired in the soft, full way that comes after a long walk. The tiredness is not a trouble. It is a kind of weight that wants to be set down, and it is already starting to slide away.

On a small table near the window stands a washbasin, a plain white bowl with a blue ring painted around its rim. The water in it has already been used. You washed your face and your hands a little while ago, and the water is cool now and quiet, with a thin skin of light on its surface. A folded cloth lies beside it, damp and soft. Your skin still carries the clean, fresh feeling of cold water, and your face is relaxed and a little cool, and it will be warm again soon.

Now lift the corner of the quilt and slide beneath it. The linen is cool for only a moment, and then it begins to warm around you. Draw the quilt up over your legs, your hips, your chest, and feel its weight settle across your body like a calm, patient hand. It does not press. It simply rests, an even, comfortable weight from your shoulders to your toes, and under it you begin to feel wrapped and held.

Lie back on the pillow. It rises to meet your head and cradles it softly. The wool beneath you shapes itself to your back. The room is warm, and the quilt is warm, and your own body adds a gentle heat that gathers in the small space between the linen and the wool. Soon you are lying in a pocket of perfect warmth, as snug as a seed in the earth, and nothing in you wants to move.

## Chapter 3: The Hush of the Fire Below

Listen, and you will notice a sound beneath the floor. It is very faint. It is not a sound that asks to be heard. It is the low, soft hush of a fire that has been banked for the night, the quiet breathing of coals in a great stone hearth, one floor below you in the common room. Even from here, you can feel the idea of it, the way you can feel the warmth of a sleeping animal in the next room.

A banked fire is the gentlest of all fires. The flames have long since settled. What remains is a bed of deep red coals, buried under a blanket of grey ash, glowing quietly in the dark. Now and then, one of the coals shifts, and a tiny whisper of ash falls from it, a sound so soft that it is more a memory than a noise. Then the hush returns, steady and slow, rising and falling like the breathing of someone asleep.

The warmth of the hearth rises through the old house the way heat rises through still water. It climbs the thick stone chimney that runs up inside the wall of your room, and the wall is a little warm to the touch, though you do not need to touch it to know. The planks beside your bed give off that quiet heat. When you breathe in, the air tastes of dry wood and old smoke and something faintly sweet, like baked apples left on a windowsill.

If you listen for the common room itself, you may catch the faintest murmur of voices, far away, no more than a warm drone like bees in a distant hive. It is the sound of a few people finishing their evening, speaking in low tones over the last of their cups. You cannot make out any words, and you do not need to. The murmur has no edges. It is only a soft, friendly texture in the air, the sound of a house with people in it, all of them content.

And then, as you listen, even that murmur begins to fade. One voice softens, and then another. A chair is pushed back, very gently, and a step crosses the floor, and a distant door closes with a small, polite click. Then the house is quieter than before, and the coals go on breathing in the hearth, and nothing else is needed. The evening has finished its talking, and the night has begun its listening.

Rest in that stillness. The tavern is asleep around you, and you are part of its sleep. The beams hold their long shadows. The stairs are dark and empty and warm. The kitchen has been scrubbed and put to rest, and the copper pots hang quietly on their hooks, each one holding a small dull gleam of the last ember light. Everything is in its place, and everything is at peace.

The hush from below is steady. It is a low, round sound, the same sound a cat makes on a warm hearthstone, a sleepy, contented sound that fills the space beneath the floor. You do not have to follow it. It follows you, in a way, rising through the boards and the quilt and the warm linen, until it becomes the quietest layer of the room, the thing the other sounds rest upon.

Notice how your breathing has begun to match it. The coals sigh, and you sigh. The ash settles, and your shoulders settle. The warmth rises, and your whole body grows warmer, heavier, and slower. There is a kind of friendship in this, between you and the glowing coals one floor down, though neither of you has said a word. You are both resting, and both keeping a quiet warmth alive through the night.

The lamp on the shelf burns low. Its small flame leans a little and straightens, leans and straightens, in time with the draught that comes through the cracks in the old plank wall. The light on the ceiling beam moves with it, a slow warm shimmer, like the light on the bottom of a shallow pool. You can watch it if you like. You can also let it drift past, and the room will stay as it is, soft and glowing and still.

Your body is heavy now, heavier than it has been all day. Your legs lie long beneath the quilt, and your arms lie loose. Your hands are warm. Your face is soft. You have come to the place in the evening where everything that was tight has quietly come undone, and what is left is only warmth and weight and slow, even breath.

## Chapter 4: Snow at the Small Window

Turn your head a little on the pillow, and look at the window. It is a small window, set deep in the thick wall, with a wooden frame painted a faded green and four small panes of old glass. The panes are not quite clear. They hold tiny ripples and little bubbles, like frozen water, and the lamplight touches them very softly, so that each pane has a faint gold glimmer in one corner.

Beyond the glass, it is snowing. The snow is falling in large, slow flakes, quite silent, drifting down through the dark like feathers shaken from a quilt. Each flake appears out of the blackness at the edge of the lamplight, turns once or twice in the air, and then falls past the window and is gone. There are so many of them, and they all move with the same gentle patience, in no hurry to arrive anywhere.

On the outer sill, the snow has gathered in a soft white ridge. It lies along the bottom edge of each pane, smooth and round, like a line of cotton laid out by a careful hand. A little has crept into the corner of the frame and melted, and left a thin silver tear that has frozen at the glass. The ridge on the sill grows by the tiniest amount with every flake, and you can almost watch it rising, as slow as a tide.

Past the window, the mountain stands in the dark. You cannot see it, but you know it is there, vast and calm, wrapped in a deep white blanket. The slopes are soft with new snow. The pines are heavy and bowed, each branch carrying a clean pale load. The valley below has gone entirely quiet, and the high road you walked this morning has been smoothed over, as though it had never been there at all.

The mountain is quiet. It is not a sleeping silence, because nothing is sleeping. It is a deep, wide silence, the kind that comes only when snow falls over a great distance and soaks up every sound. No wind moves in the trees. No river speaks beneath the ice. There is only the faint pressing hush of snow settling on snow, so soft that you hear it with the back of your mind rather than with your ears.

You are not going back out there. Let that thought settle into you. The road is behind you, and tomorrow's road is a long way off, and tonight the whole mountain has closed its door. The snow will fall until morning, and it will cover everything, and you will lie here under the heavy quilt in the warm wooden room while it does. Nothing is asked of you. Nothing is waiting beyond the glass.

The cold at the window is only a picture of cold. You can feel a thin, clean edge of it on the air near the panes, a faint coolness like the breath of a window left a crack open on a winter morning. But it stays by the glass. It does not creep across the room. The quilt keeps it away, and the wooden walls keep it away, and the coals far below keep it away, and the cold is content to remain where it is, on the other side of the glass.

There is a particular happiness in lying warm while snow falls. It is a happiness known by every creature that has ever curled up in a burrow or a nest or a quiet loft. It is the happiness of being indoors, of having walls and a roof and a bed, and of knowing that the weather can go on being weather without any need of you. You are inside that happiness now. You are wrapped in it, as softly as you are wrapped in the quilt.

Watch one flake. It comes out of the dark, slow and wide, and it touches the glass and clings there for a breath. Then it melts into a bead of water, so small that it is hardly there, and the bead runs quietly down the pane and joins the silver line below. Another flake lands beside it, and another. The window gathers them without any effort, and without any noise, and the lamp makes each one shine for a moment before it dissolves.

Your eyes are heavy now. The snow keeps falling, and the window keeps its small gold glow, and the mountain keeps its great white silence. You do not have to watch any longer. You can let your eyelids close, and the snow will go on falling behind them, soft and silent and unhurried, all through the night.

## Chapter 5: Sleep Under the Quilt

Now the room is very still. The lamp burns low. The snow falls at the window.

The quilt is heavy. It rests on your chest and your legs. It is warm, and it holds you.

The linen is clean. It is soft against your cheek. It smells of fresh air and lavender.

Your boots are by the bed. Your feet are warm. Your legs are heavy, and your arms are loose.

Breathe in. Breathe out. The air is warm and smells of wood.

The planks are warm. The beams are warm. The whole house is warm, and it is asleep.

Far below, the coals are hushing. They glow, and they rest. They glow, and they rest.

The ash is grey and soft. The hearth is quiet. The common room is dark.

The murmur has faded. The steps have gone. The doors are closed.

The latch is down. The door is shut. You are inside, and you are staying.

The snow falls on the sill. It falls on the roof. It falls on the pines and the long white slopes.

The mountain is quiet. The night is deep. The valley is still.

Your breath is slow. Your breath is long. Each one is softer than the one before.

Your hands are heavy. Your shoulders are heavy. Your jaw is soft, and your brow is smooth.

The pillow holds your head. The mattress holds your back. The quilt holds all of you.

Your thoughts are few and slow. They drift like flakes past the window, one by one, and then they are gone.

The lamp is small and gold. The flame leans, and rests.

The wood is warm. The linen is clean. The coals are hushing far below.

You are warm. You are heavy. You are sinking into the bed.

The snow is falling. The house is sleeping. The night is long.

You are safe.

You are warm.

You are safe.

Sleep.
