## Chapter One: The Lamp in the Compartment

You are already here. You are sitting in a small private compartment on an old train, and it is night. The door beside you is shut, and the latch has settled into its brass catch with a quiet, final click. Nothing needs to be done about it. The corridor beyond is hushed, and you are safe in this little room that belongs to you for the whole of the journey.

Take a slow breath, and notice the light. On the narrow table beneath the window stands a small brass lamp with a shade of pale amber glass. It gives off a warm pool of light, no wider than a dinner plate, and the glow rests on the polished wood as gently as honey resting on a spoon. The flame inside is steady. It does not flicker. It does not lean. It simply glows, the way a lamp glows in a room where nothing is wrong.

Past the edge of that warm pool, the compartment fades softly into shadow. These shadows are not dark in any unfriendly way. They are the soft brown shadows of old furniture and old velvet, the kind that gather in the corners of a well-loved room and keep it company. You can see the curve of the luggage rack above the seat, a thin line of brass catching a little amber. You can see the pale outline of a small curtain tied back beside the window. You can see your own hands resting in your lap, lit just enough to be sure they are yours.

The air in here is warm, and it carries a smell you know. It is the smell of polished wood, of wax and old varnish and many years of careful hands. Beneath it, very faint, there is the smell of tea, as if a pot was brewed in this room long ago and the scent soaked into the panels and stayed. Each time you breathe in, it is there. Each time you breathe out, the room holds it for you.

Let your shoulders drop. They have been carrying the day, and here you may set the day down. Imagine a small hook by the door, and the day hanging on it like a coat. Whatever it contained, it is only a coat now, hanging quietly, waiting for no one.

This is an old, magical train, and it has been running through the night for longer than anyone has counted. It does not keep a timetable that matters to you. It simply carries its passengers through the dark, one slow mile after another, and each passenger has a compartment like this one, with a lamp and a seat and a window. The other compartments lie far down the corridor, behind their own closed doors, and the people inside them are resting too. It is enough to know that the whole train is settling for the night, car after car, lamp after lamp.

Feel the seat beneath you. It is a deep bench, covered in thick green velvet worn soft at the edges, and it holds you the way an old armchair holds a reader. The cushion has a gentle give, and the back leans just slightly, so that your spine can rest against it without effort. Press your fingers into the velvet and feel how it springs back slowly, patient and warm. The lamplight lies across the seat, and the green glows like moss in late afternoon.

Listen, now, beneath your breathing. There is a very soft sound at the edge of the quiet, a faint murmur that seems to come up through the floor and the walls. It is the train itself, awake in the dark, carrying you along. You do not have to listen closely. It will come to you on its own.

For now, rest here in the glow. The lamp is warm. The door is shut. The wood and the tea and the old velvet are all around you, and you are held in the middle of them like a small, quiet thought. There is nowhere else you are meant to be. This compartment is where the night has placed you, and the night has chosen well.

Breathe in the smell of polished wood. Breathe out into the warm, amber light. Let the lamp stay steady beside you, as it always will, and let the room grow quieter with every slow breath.

## Chapter Two: The Rhythm of the Wheels

Now the sound comes forward, gently, the way a tide comes forward over sand. It is the sound of the wheels, rolling along the rails beneath you. It is a steady, soft rhythm, a low and patient pulse that rises through the floor and settles into the seat and into your body.

It goes like this. A long, easy beat, and another, and another. Between each beat there is a small space, a breath of quiet, and then the next beat arrives, exactly where you expected it. The rhythm never hurries. It never stumbles. It never rises into anything harsh. It is the sound of an old train that has learned over many years exactly how to carry a sleeper, and it carries you now with the same slow care.

Let the rhythm take hold of your breathing. You do not need to force it. Simply listen to the long, easy beat, and let your breath fall in beside it, the way a walker falls into step with a friend. In, with a beat and a beat. Out, with a beat and a beat. The wheels do the counting, and you can leave the counting to them.

Feel how the whole compartment sways, just a little. It is a tiny motion, a gentle leaning to one side, and then back, like a cradle moved by a patient hand. The lamp on the table does not tremble with it. The light stays calm, a warm and steady pool, and the shade of amber glass glows as quietly as before. Only the room moves, softly, and you move with it, and the lamp keeps its warmth in the middle of the motion.

Beside you on the green velvet seat lies a blanket, folded neatly into a square. You noticed it when you sat down, and it has been waiting there, patient as everything else in this room. It is a wool blanket, thick and soft, the color of oatmeal with a thin stripe of deep red along each edge. It smells faintly of cedar and of clean, dry air.

Reach for it now. Lift it onto your lap and feel the weight of it, warm from the lamp and heavy in the best way. The wool is dense and fine, with a slight roughness that is somehow comforting, like the fur of a sleepy animal. Unfold it slowly, let it open across your knees, and draw it up over your legs. Tuck the edge around you, and smooth the soft wool flat with your palm.

There. The blanket settles over you like a held breath. The warmth of the lamp is in it, and the warmth of your own body is slowly joining it, until the wool feels like a second, kinder skin. Let your hands rest on top, and feel the little ridges of the weave beneath your fingertips.

The wheels continue. Long, easy beat. Another. Another. The rhythm moves through the seat and into your back, and into the long muscles on either side of your spine, and it loosens them, one by one, as a patient hand loosens knots in a rope. You do not need to help it. The loosening happens on its own, in its own time, and the train is in no hurry to finish.

Let your eyes rest on the lamp. See how the amber light lies across the dark wood of the table, and across the folded corner of the wool blanket, and across the backs of your hands. The train moves, and the world outside moves, and the lamp does not. It is the one small steady thing in the whole moving night.

Breathe in the smell of polished wood and tea. Feel the wool warm over your knees. Hear the wheels, long and easy, soft and never hurried. Each beat is a small step further into rest. Each beat is a gentle hand saying, there, that is enough, you may let go a little more.

You let go a little more.

## Chapter Three: Fog, Forests, and Far Mountains

Turn your head, slowly, toward the window. It is a tall window with a brass frame, and the glass is cool and a little misted at the edges. Beyond the glass lies the night, deep and soft and blue-black, and through it the train is moving, steady as ever.

Outside, there is fog. It is a gentle fog, pale and slow, drifting in low ribbons across the land. It does not press against the window or hide anything that needs to be seen. It simply floats there, silvery in the faint starlight, and the train slides through it like a boat gliding over still water.

Out of the fog rise the dark shapes of trees. They are tall pines and old firs, standing close together, their branches heavy and still. They pass the window one after another, dark trunks and dark needles, sliding by in a slow and endless line. You do not need to count them. There are too many, and it does not matter. Each one is a quiet shadow, and then the next, and then the next, and they are all peaceful.

There is no danger in the fog. There is nothing in it but fog and trees and quiet. The mist is only water in the air, cool and soft, doing what mist has always done on cold nights in the woods. The forest is only a forest, old and silent and sound asleep. Every shape that passes the window is simply a shape, a dark tree or a low rock or a thin curl of vapor, and each one slides on behind you and is gone, with nothing following it.

And inside the compartment, the lamp stays steady. Look at its reflection in the window glass. There it hangs, a small warm golden light floating out among the trees, as if the lamp itself were traveling alongside the train. It trembles not at all. Behind it the dark pines go by, and in front of it your own face is only a faint outline, soft and calm.

You pull the blanket a little higher. The wool is warm against your chest, and the smell of tea and polished wood is still in the air, as it has been all along. The wheels continue their long, easy beat. Everything inside the compartment is exactly as it was, and everything outside is only a slow and silent picture, passing.

Then, very far away, something rises at the edge of the sky. It is not quite visible at first. It is only a suggestion, a place where the darkness grows a little deeper and a little heavier, and where the stars begin a little higher than before. Slowly it becomes clearer. It is a line of mountains.

They are very far off. They are only a long, soft outline against the night, with gentle shoulders and gentle peaks, and a faint silver dusting along their tops where the moon has touched old snow. They are so distant that they hardly seem to move. The train carries you slowly toward them, and slowly they remain where they are, vast and calm, like sleeping giants lying beneath a blanket of their own.

You are warm. You are dry. You are held in a small room of wood and velvet and wool, and the whole wide night is passing by outside, soft as a dream, with nothing in it that needs your attention. The forest passes. The mountains wait. The lamp stays steady. The wheels go on.

Breathe in the smell of wood and tea. Breathe out, and watch the dark trees sliding past in the fog. Each tree is a slow, quiet breath. Each breath is a little deeper than the last. And the mountains rest far away, calm and patient, as the train carries you gently through the sleeping land.

## Chapter Four: The Warm Cup

On the little table, just beside the lamp, there is a cup. You notice it now, though it has been there all along. It is a thick white cup, made of heavy china, with a wide handle and a small chip on the rim that has been softened by years of use. It sits on a saucer painted with a faint ring of blue, and a thin thread of steam rises from it, curling gently through the amber light.

It is already waiting for you. Nobody brought it. Nobody needed to. On this train, a warm cup is simply there when a traveler wants it, the way the lamp is there, and the blanket, and the soft seat. It is part of the room.

Reach out slowly, and wrap both hands around it. The china is warm, deeply and evenly warm, like a smooth stone that has lain all afternoon in the sun. Feel the heat move into your palms, and into your fingers, and slowly up through your wrists. Your hands, which may have been a little cool, begin to loosen and soften around the cup.

Bring it closer, and breathe in the steam. It is tea, dark and mild, with a trace of honey and something like warm spice, and it smells exactly like the faint sweetness that has lived in the walls of this compartment all night. The smell is soft and round. It fills your chest and settles there, the way warmth settles in a house when the fire has been lit for hours.

You do not need to drink it quickly. You do not need to drink it at all, if you would rather simply hold it. Rest your hands around the cup, and let the heat be enough. Let the steam rise past your chin, and the lamp glow through it, so that the little thread of vapor looks like a thin gold ribbon rising and fading in the air.

Or take a small sip, if you like. The tea is warm without being hot, gentle on your tongue, mild and sweet. It travels down through your chest like a quiet and friendly light, and it spreads slowly out into your arms, and into your legs beneath the blanket. Each sip is small and slow. Each sip is a soft word of comfort.

Think of the corridor outside your door. It is quiet. A long, narrow passage, carpeted in deep red, lit by small lamps set into the walls. They glow with a low and steady light, one after another, all the way down the train, and the carpet swallows every footstep and every sound. The corridor is empty, and it is still, and the lamps in it burn peacefully, keeping their watch.

Nobody knocks. Nobody has any reason to. The door of your compartment stays shut, and the latch stays in its brass catch, and the quiet outside is as soft and deep as the quiet inside. You are not waiting for anything. There is no sound in the hallway except the faint and friendly creak of the old train, and the long, slow breath of the wheels beneath the floor.

When you are ready, set the cup back on its saucer. It makes a small, low sound, china resting on china, and the sound is lost at once in the soft hum of the train. The steam still rises, slower now, thinner, a pale thread in the amber light. Your hands, empty and warm, return to rest upon the blanket.

Lean back into the velvet. Your head rests against the cushion. The lamp is steady. The wheels go on. The trees pass in the fog beyond the glass, and the far mountains wait, and the corridor lies quiet beyond the door.

Breathe in the tea. Breathe out the warmth. Let the heaviness gather in your eyelids, and let it come slowly, as the evening comes, as the steam rises, as the wheels roll on. There is nothing more to do. There is only this warm room, and this slow and gentle train.

## Chapter Five: The Coal of the Lamp

Now you may lie down. Slide along the green velvet seat, and let your head rest against the soft cushion at the end. Draw your knees up a little, or stretch them out, whichever feels kind. Pull the wool blanket up to your shoulder, and let its weight settle over you. It is warm. It is heavy. It is yours.

Or you may stay where you are, and sink back deeper into the seat, and let the velvet hold your whole back. Either way is right. The bench is wide enough, and the night is long enough, and you are comfortable.

The lamp is beside you, and its light is still warm and steady. Now it grows lower. The amber glow softens, little by little, as if the lamp itself were growing sleepy. The pool of light on the table shrinks and deepens, from gold to honey, from honey to a soft and glowing copper. The shade holds the warmth, and the room becomes dim and gentle.

The lamp is a coal now. A small red ember in a dark room. It does not flicker. It only glows, low and quiet, and the shadows come close around it, soft as wool. The wood of the walls turns the color of banked embers. The brass holds one tiny spark. The velvet is dark green, and warm.

The wheels continue. Long. Easy. Soft. The rhythm is the same as before, and it will be the same all night. It rises through the seat and through the blanket and into your back, and it rocks you gently, the way a hand rocks a cradle in the dark.

The window is a dim gray square. Beyond it, the trees pass. Slowly. Slowly. Dark shapes in the mist. A little silver in the fog. The far mountains, resting.

The smell is in the air. Polished wood. Tea. Wool. All warm. All soft.

You are heavy now. Your arms are heavy. Your legs are heavy. Your eyelids are heavy, and warm, and slow.

The train rolls on. The wheels keep their beat. Long. Easy. Soft.

The lamp glows low. A coal. A small warm ember.

The blanket holds you. The seat holds you. The night holds you.

Let the sounds grow distant. The wheels, far below. The creak of wood, far away. The soft hum of the rails, a murmur in the deep.

Let the thoughts grow quiet. They slow like the trees in the fog. They pass, and pass, and are gone.

Nothing is needed. Nothing is waiting. The train is not in a hurry. The train does not need to arrive. It carries you through the dark, one slow mile and then another, and it will go on carrying you, whether you are awake or asleep.

The door is shut. You are safe.

The lamp is low. You are warm.

The wheels are slow. You are resting.

Breathe in. The smell of wood and tea.

Breathe out. The weight of wool.

Long. Easy. Soft.

The trees slide past. The mountains wait. The lamp glows, a little coal in the dark.

You are sinking. Gently. Deeply. Like a stone into warm water. Like snow into a quiet field.

Down, into the soft. Down, into the warm. Down, into the slow and gentle dark.

The wheels go on. Long. Easy. Soft.

Sleep now. The train carries you.

Sleep now. The lamp keeps watch.

Sleep.
