## The tower

The lessons are over for the day, and you are already home. You are in the faculty tower, in the long curved corridor just past the last classroom, and the heavy door at the far end has closed behind you with a low, round sound, the way old oak doors do. Everything that needed doing today has been done. Nothing is waiting for you tonight. The only thing left is the evening itself, and it is yours.

Let your shoulders lower a little. Let your hands loosen at your sides. You are carrying a satchel of books, and it is heavier than you noticed, so you slide the strap from your shoulder and feel the weight leave you. The leather is warm from lying against your back. You hang it on an iron hook beside the door, where it swings once and then rests.

The corridor is made of old grey stone, smooth where a hundred years of hands have brushed along the walls. Every few steps there is a narrow window set deep into the wall, so deep that the sill is wide enough to sit on. Beyond each one, the snowstorm is moving. You walk to the nearest window and stand beside it. The glass is cool and a little misted at the corners. Outside, the snow is falling thickly and sideways, in long soft curtains, and below you the courtyard has already vanished under a smooth white blanket. The lamps along the garden wall are small gold smudges, blurred and gentle, and the far towers of the academy are shapes of deeper grey inside the grey air.

You can hear the storm, but only barely. It is a long, low hush, like a breath held and released. The wind leans on the tower, and the tower does not mind. These walls are thick enough to hold a whole winter outside, and they have done it for longer than anyone remembers. You are on the warm side of the stone. You are safe here, and you have always been safe here.

You begin to walk along the corridor. Your footsteps are soft, because someone laid a long runner of dark red wool down the middle of the flagstones, and it takes the sound of every step and keeps it for itself. Between the windows, lanterns hang from the ceiling on slender chains. Each one holds a small steady flame behind a pane of amber glass, and the light is the colour of honey. Under each lantern, the stone glows. Between the lanterns, it fades to shadow, and then it glows again, so you walk through a slow rhythm of warm light and soft dark, and each step makes you a little quieter inside.

There is a smell in the air, faint and welcoming, drifting up from somewhere below. It is woodsmoke, and under the woodsmoke there is something sweeter, something warm and round. You do not need to follow it yet. It will wait for you. Everything in this tower waits for you with perfect patience.

At the end of the corridor, an archway opens onto the top of a wide stone stair. Beside it there is a deep window seat with a folded blanket across it. You sit down for a moment and rest your back against the cool stone, your knees drawn up, and look out at the snow one more time. It is falling steadily through the lamplight, flake after flake, each one drifting down in no hurry at all.

There is nothing you need to listen for. There is nothing you need to remember. The day has finished, and the night has begun, and the whole tower is settling around you like a large, kind animal lowering itself to sleep. Somewhere far above, a bell gives one soft note, and the note dissolves into the snow.

You stand when you are ready. Your body is already a little heavier, a little slower, and that is just right. The stair curves gently down toward the glow of the fire, and you begin to walk toward it, one calm step at a time.

## The fire

The common room lies at the bottom of the stair, and it is warm before you even reach it. The warmth comes up to meet you, soft and steady, and it wraps around your face and your arms as you step through the low stone arch. The room is round, with a high ceiling crossed by dark beams, and every surface in it seems to have been chosen for comfort. There are deep armchairs worn smooth at the arms. There are low tables with candle stubs in clay dishes. There are rugs layered over rugs, red and brown and faded green, and shelves of well-read books with their spines gone soft.

A few other students remain, quiet and far away, tucked into corners behind open books. No one will ask you to speak.
At the centre of the far wall is the fireplace, wide enough to stand in, built of the same grey stone as the tower. The fire inside it is not large. It is a bed of deep orange coals with low flames moving gently above them. Now and then a log settles with a soft crackle and sends a few sparks drifting upward, and the sparks fade before they reach the dark of the chimney. Everything the light touches turns the colour of amber.

There is one armchair drawn up close to the hearth, with a wool blanket thrown over its arm, and you know without being told that it is yours tonight. You sink into it. The cushion takes your weight, and the back of the chair holds you exactly where you need to be held. You pull the blanket across your knees. You tuck your feet up beneath you and feel the heat of the fire on the front of your legs, gentle and even, like a hand resting there.

On the small table beside you, there is a mug, and the steam is still rising from it in a slow, thin spiral. You wrap both hands around it. The ceramic is heavy and warm, made by hand and slightly uneven. You hold it against your chest and let the warmth travel up through your wrists.

Inside, there is warm milk, pale and creamy, stirred with a spoonful of honey until the honey has vanished into it. Floating on the top, there is a single curl of orange peel, and pressed into the peel are three small clove buds, dark and fragrant. You lift the mug and breathe in. You can find the honey first, soft and golden. Then the clove, warm and quiet and faintly sharp. Then, beneath them both, the bright, gentle scent of the orange, like a small lamp in the middle of the winter.

You take a sip. The milk is hot but not too hot, and it slides down smoothly and settles low in your chest. The honey is sweet on your tongue, and the clove lingers at the back of your mouth, warm as a coal. You take another sip, more slowly. The warmth spreads from your chest into your stomach, and from there out into your arms and legs, until even your fingertips feel soft and heavy.

The fire crackles. It is the only sound in the room besides the quiet turn of a page from a far corner. Outside the tall window beside the hearth, the snowstorm continues, and its long hush comes through the glass as something far away and friendly, like a river heard from inside a house.
You watch the coals. Deep within them, a thread of red glows and fades and glows again, slow as a sleeping breath. Your eyes grow a little heavy, and you let them stay that way.

When the mug is nearly empty, you set it down gently on the table. You stay a little longer in the chair, with the blanket across your knees and the fire warming your face. There is no hurry. The bed will wait as long as you want it to.

Then, slowly, with the blanket still around your shoulders, you rise and cross the room toward the foot of the quiet stair.

## The stair

At the foot of the quiet stair, the fire's glow stretches behind you, long and golden, and your shadow lies ahead of you on the first few steps. The stair is narrow and spiral, built of pale stone worn into gentle dips at the centre of every step. Generations of tired students have climbed it at night, and they have left it smooth and kind to the feet. A rope of braided wool hangs along the wall in place of a rail, soft and slightly warm where your hand closes around it.

You begin to climb. There is no hurry in it at all. Each step is low and shallow, and your legs move easily, as if the stair were helping to carry you. The air grows a little cooler as you rise, but only a little, and the blanket around your shoulders keeps you warm. You can still smell the woodsmoke, and the faint sweetness of honey on your breath.

Every few turns, a small oil lamp sits in a niche in the wall. Each one burns with a low, round flame behind a glass chimney, and its light is soft enough to rest your eyes on.Behind you, the sound of the common room grows softer with every turn. The crackle of the fire becomes a faint, distant pop. Soon there is only the quiet of the stair itself, and the sound of your own footsteps, muffled by the thick wool of your socks. Step, and step, and step. Your breathing finds the same rhythm without your asking it to.

Halfway up, there is a narrow window set into the curve of the wall, a tall slot of old glass held in a lead frame. You stop beside it for a moment. The storm is close here. You can feel the cold coming off the pane, a faint, clean breath against your cheek. Outside, the snow is thick and slow, and it ticks softly against the glass, a tiny dry sound, tick, and tick, and a pause, and tick again, like someone tapping a fingertip very gently on a table. Beyond the glass, the night is a deep blue-grey, and the whole world is soft with snow.

It is a good sound. It is the sound of a place closed against the weather, with you inside it, and the snow outside it, and the old stone between you, steady and patient.

You climb again. The stair turns, and turns, and the air grows a little drier and warmer, because the rising heat of the tower has gathered here under the roof. Soon you can smell the sleeping floor, wool and old clean wood and sheets that have dried in a cold wind, and it comes to meet you as the common room did, gently, with no demand.

At the top of the stair, there is a short landing with a floor of wide wooden boards, dark and polished by many years of feet. They creak very softly as you cross them, like an old chair settling. Behind the doors along the landing, the other students are already asleep. The whole floor breathes quietly around you.

Your door is the one at the end. You know it by the small lamp hanging beside it, already lit, and by the way the handle is warm under your hand, because the room behind it is warm. You press down, and the door swings inward without a sound.

The room is small and round, built into the curve of the tower. The ceiling is low, with rafters of dark wood crossing it, and from one of them a single lamp hangs, glowing amber. A rug of thick grey wool covers most of the floor. A wooden chest sits at the foot of the bed, and a small desk stands beneath the window, with a candle stub and a worn book resting on it. The whole room feels like the inside of a soft, warm shell.

You step inside, and you close the door behind you, quietly, and the whole night closes with it.

## The quilt

The bed stands against the curved wall, beneath the small window, and it is waiting for you. It is a low wooden bed with a carved headboard. The mattress is deep and full, and over it lie sheets of soft, worn linen, folded back at one corner like an open hand. And over everything, spread wide and smooth, lies the quilt.

It is a heavy quilt, stitched from hundreds of small squares of wool and flannel, in deep greens and dusty blues and the brown of old leaves. The quilt is thick and slow and generous, and it rises in soft hills across the bed.

You let the blanket from the common room slip from your shoulders and lay it at the foot of the bed. You sit down on the edge of the mattress, and it takes you in gently, a deep, quiet sinking. You slide off your thick grey socks and set them beside the chest. The boards under your toes are warm from the room.

You lift the corner of the quilt and slide beneath it.

The first thing you feel is the weight. It settles over your legs, and then your stomach, and then your chest, slow and even, like a large, warm hand pressing you gently into the bed. It is the kind of weight that asks nothing of you. It only holds. Your legs grow heavy. Your hips sink into the mattress. The weight rests across your ribs, and your breath rises against it and falls away, rises and falls, and the quilt rises and falls with you.

The sheets are cool for a moment against your skin, and then they warm to the temperature of your body, and then you cannot tell where you end and the linen begins. You turn onto your side, and the pillow shapes itself to your cheek. It smells of lavender and clean cold air. You tuck your hands beneath your cheek. Your whole body is warm now, warm from the core outward, a slow, deep warmth, like the glow in a banked fire.

From where you lie, you can see the window. It is small and square, set deep in the stone, and the glass is frosted at its edges with delicate feathers of ice. Beyond it, the snow is still falling, drifting through the pale glow of a far lamp, turning and drifting, turning and drifting. The wind has softened. It no longer leans on the tower. It has become a long, low hush, no more than the sound of a sleeping animal breathing in the next room.

On the desk, the candle stub burns with one small, steady flame. Its light is gold and low, and the shadows around it are soft and deep. The whole room is the colour of honey and old wood.

You breathe in, and the air is warm, and it carries the smell of wool and cedar, and a last, faint thread of clove from your breath. You breathe out, and your shoulders melt a little further into the mattress. Your jaw has loosened. Your forehead has grown smooth. Your hands, beneath your cheek, are heavy and warm and quite still.

The weight of the quilt lies over you like a calm, deep tide, and every slow breath you take makes it a little heavier, and makes you a little softer. There is nothing to hold up. The bed is holding you. The quilt is holding you. The tower is holding you, stone upon stone, all the way down to the ground, and the storm outside cannot reach you here.

You listen to the hush of the snow. It is a gentle sound, like fine sand poured slowly from a hand, like a long breath through a quiet room. It rises a little and falls a little, and it does not need you to follow it.

Your eyes are heavy now. You let them close, just for a moment, and the warm dark behind them is soft and wide.

## Sleep

With your eyes closed, the room becomes a feeling. It is the warmth of the quilt, and the soft weight of it, and the cool, clean pillow against your cheek. You do not need to look at anything now. The room is still there, and it is still yours, and it will keep itself for you through the whole of the night.

You hear the snow. It is a faint, slow hush against the stone. Now and then a flake touches the glass, and the small tick of it is far away and unhurried. Tick. And a long quiet. And tick again. You do not need to count them. You let each one fall into the quiet and be gone.

Far below you, the fire is still burning. You cannot hear it clearly anymore. It is only a memory of a crackle, a warm orange thought at the bottom of the tower. The coals are glowing, and the embers are settling, and the chairs are empty and soft in the light. Someone will bank the fire for the night. It is taken care of. Everything is taken care of.

Your breath is slow now. It comes in, and it goes out. It comes in, and it goes out. Each breath carries a little more of the day away from you, and leaves a little more of the warmth behind.

You can still taste the honey. You can still taste the clove, a faint warmth at the back of your mouth. The orange is a soft brightness that is almost gone. It is a gentle thing to carry into sleep.

The quilt is heavy. It is so heavy and so warm. Your legs are heavy. Your arms are heavy. Your hands rest where they are, and they do not need to move.

The lamp in the rafters glows a little lower. The candle on the desk is a small gold thread. The shadows in the corners of the room are deep and soft and kind, and they hold nothing but the quiet.

The snow is falling. It falls on the roofs, and on the courtyard, and on the long grey walls of the tower. It falls on the window, and on the sill, and on the garden far below. It covers everything in white. It makes the whole world quiet.

You are warm. You are safe. You are here.

The stone holds the wind away. The wool holds the warmth close. The bed holds you.

Your thoughts are slow now. They drift and they fade. They are like the flakes outside, turning once, and falling, and gone. You do not need to follow them. You let them go.

Your breath is slow. Your body is heavy. The quilt is warm.

The room is quiet. The bed is soft. Nothing is asked of you now. Nothing is left to do. The night holds you, and the night is gentle, and you are part of its slow, warm dark.

Somewhere, a bell gives one soft note. It is very far away. The note spreads out into the snow, and the snow takes it, and the quiet comes back, deeper than before.

The hush of the storm is a lullaby now. It has no words. It has no end. It rises, and it falls, and it holds you in the dark.

Warm. Heavy. Still.

The fire is far away. The snow is soft. The honey is sweet. The clove is faint.

Your breath comes in. Your breath goes out.

The quilt is heavy. The night is long. The tower is quiet.

You are warm.

You are safe.

You are sleeping.

The snow keeps falling.

The stone keeps still.

The quilt keeps its weight.

Sleep.
