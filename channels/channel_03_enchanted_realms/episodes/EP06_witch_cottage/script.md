## Come In From the Rain

You have already come in. The door is shut behind you, the latch has dropped into its iron cradle with a small, kind click, and the heavy autumn rain that followed you along the forest path is now only a sound on the other side of the wall. You are inside a small cottage in the woods, the home of the herb-woman who lives at the end of the track, and you are warm, and dry, and entirely safe.

Stand still for a moment on the worn flagstones just inside the door, and let the place settle around you. The floor is made of flat grey stones, smooth in the middle where a great many steps have passed, and a woven rush mat lies over them, soft under your boots. The air is different in here. Outside it was cold and loud and full of wet leaves. In here it is close and quiet and full of a slow, gentle warmth, and every breath you take seems to carry some small good smell along with it.

Take off your wet cloak and hang it on the wooden peg by the door. The peg is smooth and dark, polished by years of coats, and the cloak settles on it and begins, very quietly, to drip. The drops fall on the mat one by one, a soft patient sound, like a clock that has nowhere to be. Your boots go beside the door, side by side, and your feet are in thick wool socks that somebody has left folded on the chair. They are dry, and they fit well, and your toes are glad of them.

Now look around the room, slowly, with no hurry at all. It is a small room, and every part of it is in use and in order. Low beams run across the ceiling, dark with old smoke, and from them hang bundles of dried herbs tied with thread, grey-green and soft, turning a little in the warm air. Along one wall stands a dresser crowded with jars and crocks and cups, each one a different shade of brown and cream. A basket of folded linen sits under the window. A broom leans in the corner. A small round table stands near the middle of the floor, with two chairs drawn up to it, and a plain wooden bench runs along the wall beside the fire.

You do not need greeting. The herb-woman has gone about her evening in the back room, and you can hear her now and then, a drawer sliding, a spoon touching a bowl, a low tuneless hum, as ordinary as the sounds of a house going to bed.

The window beside the door is a small square of old glass, set deep in the thick wall. The panes are a little wavy, so the world beyond them looks soft and blurred, and the rain runs down them in slow silver threads. Outside it is very dark, and very wet, and very far away. You can hear it on the thatch above your head, a steady, patient pattering, the sound of many small fingers tapping on a roof. It is a big rain, a heavy autumn rain, and it is going to go on for a long while. That is quite all right. The roof is thick, the walls are thick, and the rain has no way in. It is only a sound, and the sound is a kind one.

Let your shoulders loosen. They are still holding the shape of the cold, still lifted a little, still braced against the weather, and now they can come down. Let your jaw loosen. Let your hands hang open at your sides. The long walk through the wet woods is finished. Every step of it is behind you, left out there in the dark with the dripping branches and the cold wind, and none of it can follow you through this door.

You walk across the room, slowly, in your warm wool socks. The stones are cool through the soles, and then the rush mat is soft, and then, near the hearth, the floor begins to feel warm. It does not arrive all at once. It rises as you come closer, a gentle gathering of heat, as though the room itself is leaning in to meet you. Your cheeks feel it first, then your hands, then the front of your whole body, and the last of the outdoor chill slips quietly away, like a coat being taken off by a patient friend.

## Bread in the Oven

The warmth comes from the oven. It is built into the thick wall beside the hearth, a low arch of old brick, blackened inside and rounded like a small cave, with a heavy iron door standing a little open. Behind that door is a deep red-gold glow, steady and soft, and it is the only light in the room that matters. The rest is lamplight and shadow, but the oven is the heart of the place, and everything else gathers round it the way people gather round a quiet fire.

Sit down on the bench beside it. The wood is warm from the wall behind it, and it holds you as though it had been waiting. Settle your back against the plaster, let your legs stretch out toward the glow, and feel the warmth lay itself over your knees and shins and the tops of your feet. It is a dry, steady, generous warmth, with no edge on it anywhere. It does not burn and it does not press. It simply is, in the same way that sunshine on a stone wall simply is, patient and complete.

And there is bread in the oven. You can see two round loaves through the half-open door, sitting on a flat stone shelf, and they are the color of old honey, deeply gold on top, with a few pale cracks along the crust where they rose. They are nearly done. They have been in there for a long time, quietly becoming themselves, and now they are resting in the heat and finishing, with nothing left to do but wait.

Then the smell reaches you. It comes in slowly, the way a memory arrives, and it fills the whole room. It is the smell of warm crust, toasted and sweet and a little nutty, with the soft round smell of the inside of the loaf underneath it, like a held breath of something wholesome. It is the smell of every kitchen you have ever loved. It makes your shoulders drop another small inch. It makes your breath go slow and deep, and each long breath in is full of bread, and each long breath out is full of ease.

Listen to the oven now. It makes small sounds, the way a sleeping animal does. There is a faint tick as the bricks settle, and a low, soft murmur of heat moving through the stone, and sometimes the tiniest crackle from the crust as it cools a little and tightens. These sounds are so quiet that you would not notice them if you were not listening, and they sit just under the pattering of the rain on the roof, so that the two together make a kind of slow, double music, the soft tap above and the soft hush below.

The herb-woman comes in from the back room. She is small and unhurried, in a faded apron with flour on one sleeve, and her grey hair is tied up loosely with a bit of green cloth. She does not look at you with any surprise. She nods, once, the way one nods to a neighbor, and goes to the oven. She takes up a thick folded cloth, reaches in, and draws out one of the loaves, and the smell grows richer and warmer all at once, filling the room like a gentle tide.

She sets the loaf on a wooden board at the end of the table, where it sits steaming a little, crackling very softly, and she covers it with a clean linen cloth. She does the same with the second. Then she pushes the iron door nearly shut, leaving a narrow gold seam of light, and she dusts the flour from her hands and goes quietly back to whatever she was doing, humming her low tuneless song.

You do not need to eat. The loaves can cool on their board under their cloth, and the smell can go on filling the air, and you can simply sit here, in the glow of the oven, with the warmth soaking into your bones.

The oven glows. The loaves rest. The rain taps on the roof, and the whole small cottage seems to breathe, slowly, in and out, with you inside it like a quiet word held in a warm mouth. You lean your head back against the wall, and your eyes are half closed, and you watch the red-gold seam of the oven door, steady and low, steady and low.

## Chamomile on the Table

After a while a cup appears beside you. You did not see it come. The herb-woman has set it down on the corner of the table nearest the bench, and she has gone away again as quietly as she arrived, leaving only the faint creak of a floorboard behind her. It is a plain cup, thick and round, glazed a soft cream color with a small chip on the rim, and it has a flat saucer beneath it, and a thin curl of steam is rising from it into the lamplight.

Reach out and take it in both hands. The cup is warm all the way around, warm like a small living thing, and the warmth goes into your palms and up through your wrists and into your arms. Hold it close to your chest for a moment before you do anything at all. Feel the weight of it, solid and comfortable, and the way the steam touches your chin and the underside of your nose.

It is chamomile. You can tell by the smell, a light, sweet, green smell, like a meadow in the afternoon, with a faint note of apples somewhere behind it. It is only a warm flower smell, soft and clean and simple, and it asks nothing of you. You do not need to know anything about it. You do not need to think about what it does or does not do. It is a warm cup on a cold night, and the flowers in it were picked in the summer and dried on those beams above your head, and now they are giving back a little of that summer, quietly, one slow thread of steam at a time.

Look into the cup. The tea is the pale gold of late afternoon light, clear and still, with a few small flowers drifting near the bottom, tiny and round, like little suns that have gone down for the evening. The surface trembles very slightly as you breathe, and the lamplight makes a soft circle on it, and the circle trembles too.

Lift the cup to your lips and take the smallest sip. It is warm, not hot, and it tastes gently of flowers and honey, and a little of the clay of the cup itself. It slides down warm, and you feel it settle somewhere behind your ribs, a small round glow that matches the glow of the oven. You hold the sip for a moment, and let it go, and take a breath through your nose, and the smell is there again, sweet and green and quiet.

Take another sip, if you like, in your own time. Or only hold the cup, and let it warm your hands. There is no right way. The tea will be there as long as you want it, and when it cools it will be just as kind. The table is solid beside you, the bench is warm beneath you, and the lamp throws a soft yellow circle over the wood grain and the corner of the linen cloth and the two golden loaves resting under their cover.

The rain is a little softer now, or perhaps you are only hearing it more deeply. It is a steady patter, steady and unhurried, and now and then a larger drop falls from the edge of the thatch onto the stone outside with a small round sound, like a bead dropped into a bowl. The wind has dropped almost to nothing. The trees in the dark are quiet. The whole forest outside is drinking, slowly, the way a garden drinks after a long dry summer, and it has no need of you, and you have no need of it.

Your hands are warm. Your arms are warm. Your chest is warm, and the warmth goes down into your belly and settles there like a cat settling into a basket. The tea cools a little in the cup, and you drink a little more, and the little white flowers turn slowly in the gold. The room smells of bread and flowers and old wood, and each of those smells is a small soft hand laid on your shoulder.

You set the cup down on the saucer with a quiet clink, and you let your hands rest in your lap. They are warm and loose and a little heavy. The tea has done nothing but be warm, and that is the whole of what it ever needed to do. You sit back, and the bench holds you, and the oven glows, and the rain taps on, and you sink one more small step into the quiet of the cottage.

## A Cat in Your Lap

There is a small sound on the floor beside you, the softest sound, the sound of paws on a woven mat. You look down and a cat is there, a round, soft, grey-and-white cat with a dark nose and half-closed amber eyes, looking up at you with great calm. It does not hurry. It does not ask. It only considers your lap for a moment, in the way a cat considers a good chair, and then it gathers itself and steps up, one paw and then the other, onto your knees.

It turns around once, slowly, and then again, treading gently on the wool of your sleeve, and then it folds itself down with a long, easy sigh and settles. You feel the weight of it arrive. It is not much weight, and yet it is exactly the right weight, warm and living, pressing softly into your thighs, like a hot water bottle that breathes. Your hand finds its back without any decision at all. The fur is thick and soft, cool at the tips and warm underneath, and it moves smoothly beneath your palm.

Stroke it slowly, from the head down to the base of the tail. Feel the small ridges of its spine, and the loose soft skin of its shoulders, and the faint warm rise and fall of its breath. And now it begins to purr. It begins very quietly, like a far-off engine, a low, even rumble that you feel in your hand and in your legs before you really hear it. The purr grows, and finds its steady pace, and stays there, round and slow and endless.

Let the purr in. It is a lovely, lazy sound, and it seems to come from everywhere at once, from the cat and from your knees and from the warm bench and from the walls of the cottage themselves. It mixes with the soft hush of the oven and the small tap of the rain, and the three of them blend together until you cannot tell where one stops and another begins. Rain, and oven, and purr. Rain, and oven, and purr.

The cat's weight sinks deeper. Its body grows heavier and looser as it relaxes, and the warmth of it spreads through the wool of your lap and into your legs, and you feel yourself being held down gently, as though by a kind and living blanket. Its paws tuck beneath its chest. Its tail curls around itself. Its eyes close, and then half open, and then close again, and the purr goes on, undisturbed, as steady as a heartbeat.

You rest your hand on its side and feel the breath go in and out, and you let your own breath match it. In, and out. In, and out. The two of you breathe together in the lamplight, with the oven glowing beside you and the loaves cooling under their cloth. The rain on the roof is kinder now, softer, a thin gentle whisper of water on straw, no longer a heavy patter but a long, quiet murmur, like someone telling a very old story in a very low voice.

The lamp burns on the table, and a few dried flowers turn on their threads from the beams, and the shadows in the corners of the ceiling are deep and soft and still.

Nothing here is waiting for you. Nothing is wanted from you at all. You are a guest in a small warm house, with a cat on your lap and tea in your belly and bread cooling on the table, and the rain outside is only a sound, a kind, kind sound, going on and on.

## The Purr Stays

The rain is soft on the roof. The oven glows.

You are warm. The cat is heavy and warm in your lap, and its purr is low and slow.

Your hand rests on its fur. Your hand is still. Your breath is long.

There is a narrow bed near the oven, in the corner, with a patchwork quilt folded at the foot, and a pillow that smells of lavender and clean linen. Or there is the chair, deep and old, with a cushion at your back. It does not matter which. You are already in one of them, and the blanket is already over your knees, and the cat is already on your lap. The quilt is warm. The pillow is soft. You sink into it.

The bread is cool on the board. The cup is empty on the saucer. The lamp is turned low, and the room is gold and brown and quiet.

The rain taps, and taps, and softens. It is a whisper now. It is a hush on the thatch. It is a long low breath over the roof, in, and out, in, and out.

The oven ticks. The bricks settle. The red seam of light is steady and low, and it lies across the floor like a thin warm ribbon, reaching almost to your feet.

The loaves are cool now, and the smell of them is soft and faint, a gentle sweetness left in the air. The dried herbs turn on their threads, one slow turn, and then still. The cup holds the last small warmth of the flowers. The window is a dark square, and the rain on the glass is a faint silver thread, running slowly down and out of sight.

The cat purrs. Round and slow. Round and slow.

Its weight is warm on your lap. Its breath rises and falls beneath your hand. You are heavy, and it is heavy, and the chair, or the bed, holds you both, and the old walls hold the chair, and the dark forest holds the walls, and the night holds all of it, gently, without any effort at all.

Your eyes are closed. Your shoulders are heavy. Your hands are heavy. The warmth is all around you, and it is deep, and it is kind.

The herb-woman hums in the back room. The sound is far away. It is only a thread of tune. It is only a thread.

The rain is only a sound. The sound is soft. The sound is far.

The oven glows. The bread rests. The tea is warm in you still.

The cat purrs.

Round, and slow.

Round, and slow.

You rest.

The purr stays.
