## The Quiet Dome

You are already inside. The door has closed behind you, and the last soft sound of it settled a long while ago. Nothing is left to do now. The day has been put away, and the night in the greenhouse has begun, and you are part of it.

You stand on a narrow path of packed pale gravel, and the gravel is warm under your wool socks. Each step makes a small, patient crunch, and then the crunch is gone, and the air takes it. You are wearing a loose gray shirt and soft trousers, the kind of clothes that forget they are on you. Your shoulders are low. Your hands are open at your sides. Your breath comes in through your nose, slow and easy, and leaves again without any effort at all.

Above you, the dome rises in one long curve. It is made of clear panels set into pale ribs, and the ribs meet far overhead at a quiet center, like the spokes of a great, still wheel. Every panel is whole. Every seam is tight and calm. The dome has stood here through many nights, and it stands here tonight, round and steady, holding the warmth in and holding the quiet in, the way a cupped hand holds a small flame.

The light in here is low. The tall daytime lamps have been turned down to a faint amber glow, and the glow sits in the leaves and on the wet stones and along the edges of the beds. There is no hurry in it. It is the color of honey held up to a window, and it makes everything slightly softer than it was by day. The sharp corners of the benches look rounded. The metal rails look like old brass. Even the tools, hung in a neat row beside the door, look like sleeping things, resting on their hooks.

The outpost around this greenhouse is quiet too. The people who live here have gone to their rooms, one by one, and the low buildings beyond the dome are dim and still. Nobody needs you tonight. Nobody is waiting for you to answer anything. The work of the day was finished, and it was good work, and it is behind you like a field already harvested.

You walk a little farther along the path, and you let yourself notice how your body feels. Your feet are warm. Your legs are heavy in a pleasant way, the way they are after a long, useful day. Your lower back is loosening, a little more with each step. Your jaw is soft. The small muscles around your eyes have stopped holding anything. You do not have to arrange your face for anyone. There is no one here to see it, and the plants do not mind how you look.

The air is gentle. It is a little damp, and it carries the smell of soil and green stems and water moving over stone. It is the air of a place where things grow, and you breathe it in and feel it settle all the way down into your chest. Breathe in slowly, and let it out slowly. The dome breathes with you, in its own quiet way, the leaves lifting and lowering by a hair as the gentle air moves among them.

There is a faint hum in the floor, so low that you feel it more than you hear it. It is the sound of the greenhouse looking after itself, the water moving in its hidden channels, the soft machinery that keeps this room kind. You have heard it so many nights that it has become a kind of lullaby. It does not ask for your attention. It only stays, steady and low, like the purr of something sleeping near the fire.

The path bends to the left, and you follow it, and the dome seems to bend with you. The ribs overhead slide past one by one, each pale curve crossing the dark glass and then vanishing behind your shoulder. You are in no hurry to reach the end of it. Walking is enough. The slow, even rhythm of your steps is enough. Left foot, then right, then left again, and the warm gravel whispers beneath you.

Somewhere ahead, in the amber dimness, you can hear water. It is a tiny sound, a little silver thread of sound, and it comes and goes as the path turns. You are walking toward it, the way a person walks toward a lit window. You do not need to know exactly where it is. The sound will lead you, gently, the whole way.

The dome is intact, and the night is quiet, and you are warm. That is all there is to say about it. You are home, inside the glass, and the rest of the world can be as large and as silent as it likes.

## The Trees and the Stream

The path opens into the middle of the greenhouse, and here the trees begin.

They came from Earth, long ago, as small seedlings wrapped in damp cloth, carried carefully from hand to hand. Now they stand tall and broad in the soft amber light, their trunks thick and brown, their branches spreading high into the curve of the dome. There is an oak with deep furrows in its bark. There is a birch, pale and slender, its white trunk glowing faintly in the lamplight. There is a maple whose wide leaves overlap like a hundred green hands resting on one another. Between them stand smaller trees, apple and plum and pear, and under them grow ferns and low shrubs and soft cushions of moss.

You stop beneath the oak and rest your palm against its trunk. The bark is rough and warm, and it holds the faint, living warmth of a thing that has been growing for years. You can feel the grooves under your fingers, each one a small valley, each one worn smooth by time. You rest your forehead lightly against the wood and breathe in. It smells of rain on dry earth and of a forest floor in autumn, and it smells of something older than words, something your body remembers without being told.

This is the smell of home. Not the smell of a house, and not the smell of any one place you have lived, but the deeper smell that all those places had in common. Damp leaves. Dark soil. Green stems bruised gently by a passing sleeve. You breathe it in again, and your chest rises, and then it falls, and the smell stays with you for a long moment after the breath is gone.

You walk on, beneath the branches. Leaves hang low here, and you let them brush your shoulders as you pass. They are cool and slightly damp, and they make a faint dry whisper against your shirt. A few have fallen to the path, and they lie there in soft brown and gold, curled at their edges like small sleeping animals. You step around them, or over them, or sometimes onto them, and they give the quietest crackle under your feet and then are still.

The sound of the water grows clearer now, and soon you find it.

The stream begins at a little rock pool near the foot of the maple, where water rises gently from somewhere beneath the stones. It spills over a lip of dark rock and runs in a narrow, winding channel through the whole length of the greenhouse. It is no wider than your two hands side by side. It is shallow and clear, and in the amber light it looks like a ribbon of slow honey, with silver threads running through it.

You kneel beside it. The stones at the edge are smooth and round, and a thin film of moss grows on them, the green of old velvet. You dip your fingers into the water, just the tips, and it is gentle and cool and welcoming. It slides around your knuckles and carries on, in no hurry, toward the far end of the dome. You lift your hand, and the drops fall from your fingertips back into the stream, each one making a tiny, perfect ring that widens and fades.

Listen to the sound of it. It is not loud. It is a low murmur and a faint trickle, a whisper that rises a little over a pebble and falls again in the next hollow. It is the sound of water that has all the time in the world. It speaks without words, and it says the same thing over and over, softly, a single reassuring sentence that you do not have to understand. Go slowly. Go gently. Keep going.

Ferns line the banks, their fronds arched over the water like the roofs of tiny houses. Their tips are damp, and every so often a drop gathers at the end of one and falls into the stream with a little plink. Moss cushions the stones, thick and springy and deeply green. Small white flowers grow in the shadows, their petals closed for the night, each one folded up like a sleeping hand.

You stay here a while, kneeling beside the water, and you breathe. The smell of wet stone mixes with the smell of leaves, and the smell of leaves mixes with the faint sweetness of the apple blossom somewhere above you. The air feels soft against your skin. It is the kind of air that does not need to be thought about. It simply is, and you simply are, and the two of you are comfortable together.

Slowly you stand again, and your knees give a small, sleepy creak, and you smile at it. You follow the stream along its winding course, walking beside it on the soft earth. It curves around the roots of the birch and slips beneath a little wooden footbridge, where the planks are worn smooth and gray. You cross the bridge slowly, listening to the water go by beneath your feet. Halfway across, you stop and rest your hands on the railing, and you simply listen.

Leaves rustle faintly above. Water murmurs below. The lamp glow lies over everything like a warm, soft cloth. There is nothing here that you need to do, and nothing that you need to be. There is only the green quiet of the greenhouse, and you, a part of it, at ease among the trees.

## Beyond the Glass

At the far side of the greenhouse, the trees thin out, and the path leads you to the edge of the dome, where the glass comes down nearly to the ground.

Here the amber light from the lamps fades, and the world outside begins. You stop a step or two from the glass and stand quietly, your hands loose at your sides, and you look out.

The sky is dark. It is a deep, clear, velvet dark, without a single cloud in it. The stars are scattered across it in great numbers, some bright and steady, some so faint that they seem to appear only when you do not look straight at them. They shine without flickering. They sit in the dark like tiny lamps left burning in a very large room. Look at them for a while, and you will find that the dark between them is not empty. It is soft, and it is deep, and it holds the stars the way a quiet pond holds the reflection of a lantern.

Below the sky lies the land. It stretches away from the dome in every direction, wide and low and utterly still. Its color is rust. It is the red-brown of old iron and baked clay, the color of a clay pot left out in the late afternoon. In the dim starlight the shade deepens, and the whole wide plain looks like a slumbering animal, curled up under a blanket of fine dust. There is no movement on it. Nothing stirs. The dust lies exactly where it lay when you last looked, smooth and even, with faint ripples in its surface like the lines on a sleeper's palm.

Rocks lie scattered here and there across the plain. Some are small and round, some are broad and flat, and some are tall and uneven, like the stumps of old towers. Each of them casts a long, soft shadow across the sand, a shadow as quiet as the rock itself. The shadows all lean the same way, as though the whole land were leaning gently to one side in its sleep.

In the distance, a low ridge rises along the edge of the world. It is a long line of darker rust against the darker sky, rolling and smooth, like a blanket pulled up over a sleeping shoulder. Beyond it there is only the sky again, and the stars, going on and on.

Close to the dome, you can see the ribs of the glass laid out in faint lines across the sand. The starlight and the amber glow from within make a pattern there, a soft web of light and shadow, curving gently outward. It looks like the shell of some great, gentle creature, and in a way it is. The dome shelters you, and the plants, and the stream, and the lamps, and all of it sits inside one calm curve, safe and bright in the dark.

You rest your hand lightly against the glass. It is smooth and clear and perfectly still. You can see your own faint reflection in it, a soft shape against the amber light, and past that shape you see the plain and the stars. For a moment, the two seem to overlap, you and the quiet land, as though you were standing inside the night itself and the night did not mind.

There is a small, steady light on the edge of the outpost, a tiny orange glow on a distant post. It does not move. It does not flash. It simply burns, small and constant, like a candle in a window at the end of a long road. It tells you that the world is where it should be, and that all is quiet, and that someone has left a light on for the night.

The silence outside is complete. You cannot hear it through the glass, but you can feel it, the way you feel the stillness of a snowy field or an empty church. It is wide and soft and deep. It is not an empty silence, nor a hungry one. It is a resting silence, like the pause between two slow breaths, the pause that holds everything gently before the next breath begins. The land is not waiting for anything. It is simply being still, and being still is enough for it.

You stand there for a long while and let the quiet settle on you. The rust-colored plain lies below. The stars hang above. The glass holds them both at a gentle distance, and the amber light behind you holds you. You are in the exact middle of it, warm and small and perfectly looked after.

Your eyelids feel heavier now. Your breath has slowed. You let your gaze drift once more across the stillness, over the dust and the rocks and the long soft ridge, and then you let your eyes close for a moment, and you listen to the faint murmur of the stream behind you, calling you gently back toward the trees.

## The Cot Beneath the Tree

You turn from the glass and walk back along the path, and the amber light comes up around you like warm water.

Beneath a small apple tree, near the bend of the stream, stands a low cot. It has been set there for nights like this one. Its frame is simple, made of smooth pale wood, and its mattress is thick and soft, covered with a worn canvas that has been washed so many times it feels like cloth from a dream. At its foot lies a folded blanket, heavy and wide, woven from soft gray-green wool.

A small lamp stands on a flat stone beside the cot. Its shade is made of old paper, and the light inside it is the warmest in the whole dome, a low golden glow that spills over the stone and the grass and the edge of the blanket. It does not flicker. It simply shines, steady and kind, like a hearth in a quiet room.

You sit on the edge of the cot, and the frame gives a soft, creaking sigh beneath you. You sit for a moment with your hands on your knees. Then you slip off your socks and set them neatly by the leg of the cot. The grass beneath your bare feet is short and soft and a little damp, and it feels wonderful. You curl your toes into it, and then you let them go.

You lie back slowly. The mattress holds you, and the cot holds the mattress, and the earth holds the cot. A pillow, small and flat and filled with dried grass, rests under your head, and it smells faintly of summer. You settle your shoulders against the canvas, and you let your arms rest at your sides. The long muscles of your back loosen, one after another, like knots being untied in a rope.

You draw the blanket up over your legs, and your hips, and your chest. It is heavy, and it is warm, and it settles over you like a hand resting on your shoulder. The wool is a little rough against your fingers and soft against your chin. You pull it up just beneath your jaw and tuck your hands beneath it, and the warmth gathers around you, close and patient.

Above you, the branches of the apple tree spread out like a slow, quiet roof. Leaves hang from them in layers, each one edged in amber from the lamp. Between the leaves, you can see small pieces of the dome beyond, the pale ribs curving away, and past the ribs a few of the stars, shining faintly through the glass. You can see them, and they cannot touch you. They are far away and bright and peaceful, like lanterns carried slowly past on a faraway road.

The stream runs beside your head. It is only an arm's length away, and its whisper is clear and close. It murmurs over its stones and slips around its bends, and its voice rises and falls gently, never quite the same, never quite stopping. You listen to it, and you let it fill the space inside your head, so that there is room for nothing else. Water over stone. Water around root. Water passing on.

The smell of the leaves drifts down over you. Damp leaves, and green stems, and the faint sweetness of the fruit trees. It is the smell of the inside of the dome, and the smell of rest, and the smell of every good night you have ever had, folded together and laid gently over your face.

The lamp glows beside you. Its warm light touches your hands, your cheek, the edge of the blanket, and then slips away into the dim green space around you. It is enough light to feel safe by, and not so much that your eyes must work. It is the light of a small, steady fire, the light of a window at dusk, the light of someone who has promised to stay up a little longer so that you do not have to.

Outside, beyond the glass, the rust-colored plain lies silent under the stars. You know it is there, the same as it was, smooth and wide and still. You cannot hear it. You do not need to. Its silence is a soft thing, a deep and restful thing, like a great, quiet field in the hours before dawn. It does not press against the glass. It simply rests there, at a gentle distance, and it keeps you company without a single word.

You feel the stillness inside you now, and the stillness outside you, and they feel like the same stillness, shared. Your breath rises and falls. The blanket rises and falls with it. The leaves above you move by the smallest breath, and the stream speaks beside you, and the lamp holds the dark away in a soft, warm circle.

You have nowhere to go, and nothing to do. You are exactly where you are meant to be.

## Sleep

Now you can rest.

The lamp is warm. The blanket is warm. The cot holds you. The tree is over you. The stream is beside you, and it keeps on.

You breathe in. You breathe out.

Your hands are heavy. Your arms are heavy. Your legs are soft and still. The warmth moves through you, slowly, from your feet to your knees, from your knees to your hips, from your hips to your chest. It settles there. It stays.

The leaves smell green. The air smells soft. The stream says its quiet word, and says it again.

You are inside. The dome is whole. The glass is calm. The night is outside, and you are in here, and that is how it should be.

Beyond the glass, the stillness is rust-colored and wide. The stars are slow and bright. The plain does not move. It does not need to. It sleeps, and you sleep, and the glass holds you both.

You breathe in. You breathe out.

Your face is soft. Your jaw is loose. Your brow is smooth. Your eyes are heavy, and they close, and the lamp glows through your eyelids like a small gold sun, far away.

The stream runs on. It runs over the stones. It runs around the roots. It runs under the little bridge, and past the birch, and on through the ferns, to the end of the dome, and it begins again.

The blanket is a warm weight. The cot is a quiet shore. The tree is a green roof. The lamp is a small, kind light.

You are safe. You are warm. You are here.

Breathe in. Breathe out.

A leaf turns, once, and is still.

The stream runs on, and on.

The air is soft. The dark is soft. The sky is wide beyond the glass, and the glass is whole, and you are inside.

Your thoughts are slow now. They drift like leaves on the water, one by one, turning gently, passing by. You do not need to follow them. You let them go, and the stream carries them, and they are gone.

Your body is heavy and warm. The cot holds it. The earth holds the cot. Nothing is asked of you.

You breathe in. You breathe out.

The lamp is warm. The stream is near. The dome is quiet.

Sleep comes, and you let it.

The stream runs on.

The leaves are still.

The glass is clear.

The rust-colored night rests outside, and you rest inside, and the small lamp keeps its gentle gold.

You are asleep.

The stream runs on.
