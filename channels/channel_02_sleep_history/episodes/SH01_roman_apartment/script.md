Hello. If you are still awake, you can set the day down here. Nothing in this story will startle you, and nothing needs an answer. This is a quiet night in an ordinary Roman apartment, in a crowded city, a long time ago. You can close your eyes whenever you like. The room will go on without you.

We are not going to a palace, and we are not going to a battle. We are going up a stair, into a small room, and we are going to stay there while the street grows quieter. If a detail is uncertain, it will be marked and then set gently aside. The rest is the ordinary evening of a city that has already done its work.

## The stair

The building is called an insula, which simply means an island. In a Roman city, a great many people did not live in a house with a courtyard and a garden. They lived in apartments, stacked one above another, with shops along the street at the bottom and rooms above them. From the outside, in the last light, it is a tall, plain face of brick and timber, with small openings and a doorway at street level. Someone has already closed the shop. The wooden front is shut. The smell of the day's trade, grain or oil or bread, is still in the air, but it is fading.

You step out of the street and into the bottom of the stair. The stair is narrow. The wood has been climbed by many feet, and it gives a small, familiar sound under you. It is not a grand staircase. It turns, and it turns again, and the light from the street thins behind you. Your hand can find the wall. The wall is cool. Down on the pavement, people are still walking home. Their voices come up the stairwell as a soft blur, not as words you need to catch.

On the lower floors, the rooms are usually the better ones. Higher up, the rooms grow smaller, and the climb is longer, and the rent is easier to bear. That is the ordinary shape of a building like this. You are going up, not all the way to the top, just high enough that the street is a little farther off. At a landing you pass a door that is already shut. Behind it, someone moves a bowl, or sets down a pair of shoes. You do not knock. This is not your door. The building holds many separate evenings, close together, each one minding itself.

There is a smell of olive oil and of warm dust. Someone cooked earlier, somewhere below, and the ghost of that meal is still in the stair. Cooking in these upper rooms was not always simple, and many people ate food that had been prepared down at street level, or kept their fire very small. [FACT-CHECK] You do not need the exact rule. What you can trust is the feeling of the stair: a lived-in smell, a tired smell, not an unpleasant one. Bread. Oil. The faint trace of smoke that has already thinned.

A window on the landing has a wooden shutter. It is half open. Glass was a luxury, and a small upper window like this was more often closed with wood than with a pane. [FACT-CHECK] Through the gap you can see a strip of sky between roofs. The sky is not a spectacle. It is a deep blue going toward black, with a few steady stars. No one is asking you to name them. They are simply there, above the tiles, the way they are above your own roof.

You pause on the last steps, not from weariness so much as from the pleasure of arriving. The wood under your foot is worn smoother in the middle, where everyone walks. A knot in the board meets the sole and then lets go. Somewhere below, a broom makes one slow pass and stops. The person sweeping is finished too. You do not look down the well of the stair to see them. The sound is enough, and then the sound is gone.

You reach the door you came for. The latch is ordinary. Inside, the room is already dimmer than the landing. You close the door until it sits in its frame. The sound of the latch is the sound of the day agreeing to stop.

## The lamp

The room is not large. There is space for a bed, for a chest or a shelf, for a stool, and for the small business of washing and folding. The walls have been plastered, and the plaster is a quiet color, uneven if you look closely, smooth enough under the hand. The floor is hard. A mat lies where people stand most often. Nothing here is trying to impress a guest. It is a room that knows its own person.

On a shelf, or on a low stand, there is an oil lamp. It is clay, with a little body for the oil and a narrow neck where the wick comes out. A flame this small does not light the whole room at once. It makes a pool. The pool is warm at the center and soft at the edges, and beyond the edges the corners of the room stay gentle and dark. You can leave them dark. There is nothing in the corners that you need.

The lamp itself is a humble shape, the kind a potter made by the dozen, with a thumbprint still hiding in the clay if the light were stronger. You do not turn it over to look. It is doing its work.

Olive oil feeds the flame. The smell is clean and a little nutty, the smell of a kitchen at rest. The wick has been trimmed. Someone did that with the same care you use when you turn a switch and then turn it lower. The flame stands up, leans, and stands again. It is a small, repeating motion, easy for the eyes to follow until the eyes would rather not follow anything.

Near the lamp there might be a jug of water, covered, and a cup. Water for a room like this was often carried from a public fountain. You can hear, if you want to, the memory of that errand: the weight of the jug, the cool of the stone basin, the short walk home before the last light went. The water is for the hands, for the mouth, for the ordinary thirst of evening. You do not have to drink. It is enough to know the jug is there.

The shutter at the room's window is almost closed. A line of air comes through. The air is city air, but at this hour it is cooler than it was at midday. It moves the flame just enough to prove the room is not sealed away from the world. Then the flame settles. Outside, a cart may pass. In Rome, heavy wagons were kept out of the streets by day, so some of their noise belonged to the night. [FACT-CHECK] If you hear wheels, they are far enough below that they sound like a slow roll, not like a crash. They come, they pass, and the street takes them away.

You sit, or you stand by the lamp a moment longer. The day has left a few objects out of place. A cloak over the stool. A pair of sandals near the mat. A wooden comb. None of these things ask to be put right this minute. Morning can have them. The lamp does not mind a little disorder. It only lights what is close.

If you listen inward, toward the other rooms, you hear the building the way a person hears a house settling. A footstep over your head, unhurried. A murmur that might be a conversation and might only be someone talking to a child, or to themselves, about nothing important. These sounds are the proof that you are not alone in the dark, and also the proof that no one needs you to answer. The walls are thin enough for life to leak through, and thick enough for that life to stay its own.

## Bread and water

Suppose the meal is already over. For most people the important meal came late in the day, and by now the hunger has been met. What remains is small. A piece of bread. Perhaps a little cheese, or the last of a lentil stew, grown quiet and thick as it cooled. Olive oil in a shallow dish. Salt. These are not the foods of a banquet. They are the foods that make a room smell like someone lives here on purpose.

Bread in this city is a daily fact. It is baked in ovens that serve many households, and it comes home warm or already cool, wrapped in a cloth or simply carried. The crust is firm. The inside is pale. If you break a piece, the sound is soft, and a few crumbs fall to the table or to the broad rim of a bowl. You can leave the crumbs. In the morning they will be swept, or a bird on the sill will take an interest if the shutter is opened. Tonight they are only crumbs.

There might be fruit, if the season allows it. A fig, or a handful of grapes gone a little soft. You do not need to decide which. The point is the sweetness that stays on the tongue for a moment and then becomes nothing but the wish for water. The water in the jug is not cold the way ice is cold. It is cool the way clay is cool, the way a cellar is cool. It tastes faintly of the vessel. That taste is part of the evening.

You wash your hands. The water runs over the fingers and into a basin. The little noise of pouring is one of the kindest sounds a room can make. Then the basin is set aside. The towel is rough linen, clean enough, used often. Your hands are your own again, empty of the day's work.

Somewhere in the building, a door opens and closes. Not loudly. A greeting is exchanged on the stair, two voices that recognize each other, and then the stair is only a stair. You do not get up to see who it was. The lamp keeps your attention in this smaller circle. If you were to speak, you would speak softly, because the walls share sound, and because the hour has earned a lower voice.

The city outside is changing its clothes. Shops are dark. The forum, if you could see it from here, would be a wide empty space with a few lamps and a few late walkers, but you cannot see it, and you do not need to. Your window gives you roofs, a lane, and that strip of sky. The stars are a little clearer than they were when you were on the landing. They do not form a lesson. They are a scattered quiet.

People in the street talk less now. What you hear instead is the building itself. Timber answers the cooling air with a small tick. Someone upstairs sets a lamp down, and the ceiling answers with one patient knock. A dog, far away, says one word and then forgets why. None of this builds toward anything. It is the ordinary weather of a night.

If you are listening from your own bed, you can borrow this pace. The Roman room is not asking you to remember dates. It is offering you a sequence you already know from your own life. Eat. Wash. Sit. Let the light be small. Let the larger city become a rumor. The centuries between that room and yours do not have to be crossed with effort. A lamp is a lamp. A jug is a jug. A person at the end of a day is a person at the end of a day.

You cover what is left of the bread. You set the cup near the jug so that a hand can find it later without hunting. These are the last useful gestures. After them, usefulness can rest.

## The bed

The bed is a simple frame, or a couch drawn close to the wall, with a mattress that has been shaken and turned many times. The stuffing is whatever a household could afford and replace, wool or straw or a mix that has taken the shape of the person who sleeps here. It is not a display. It holds the heat of the room and a little of your own heat once you lie down. A cover lies folded at the foot, wool, a little heavy, the right weight when the night air finds the gap at the shutter.

You loosen your sandals and leave them where the morning can find them. The floor is cool under the foot for one step, and then the mat, and then the edge of the bed. Sitting first is enough. The lamp is still burning. You do not have to blow it out yet. Many people let a lamp live a while longer, a small company, until sleep is already near. The flame uses its oil slowly. There is enough.

From the bed you can see the window slit and the star in it, if a star has agreed to stand in that narrow place. It does not twinkle for your benefit. It only shines the way a distant lamp shines, without smoke and without a wick. Between that light and the oil flame, the room is held. You are held with it.

The sounds keep their order. A voice somewhere becomes a hum, and the hum becomes part of the wall. The wheels, if they come again, roll and are gone. Your own breath is closer than any of them. You can follow it down and up, without counting, the way you have followed it all your life. The mattress answers each breath with a very small change of shape. That is all the motion the room requires.

If another person shares this room, they are already on their way to sleep, or they are still, the way people are still when they have decided the day is finished. You do not need to imagine their story. It is enough that the bed is a bed and the night is the same night for everyone under this roof. The insula, the little island, keeps its many sleepers. Each room is its own island inside the larger one.

The flame is shorter now. You can let it go on until it goes out by itself, or you can pinch the wick and leave a curl of smoke that smells of oil and then of nothing. Either way, the dark that follows is not empty. It is the same room, learned by touch. The jug. The folded cover. The shutter. The door that is latched.

Down in the street, the city continues at a walk. It will be there in the morning, with bread again, and fountains, and the stair to climb. You do not have to meet it yet. Morning is a different room, and it can wait outside the door.

Here, the cover is up to the shoulder. The pillow, if there is a pillow, is only a folded cloth, and it is enough. The last star in the shutter gap does not ask to be named. Your eyes can close. The building settles one more time, a soft tick in the timber, and then it too is finished with movement.

The night goes on. The story does not need an ending beyond that. The lamp is quiet. The street is farther away than it was. If you are still listening, let the next breath be longer than the one before it, and the one after that longer still. No one will call you back. You are allowed to sleep.
